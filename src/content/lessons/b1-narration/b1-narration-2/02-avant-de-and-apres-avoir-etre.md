# Avant de and Après avoir/être: Sequencing Without a Second Clause

The plus-que-parfait handles sequencing across two full clauses. But French also has two compact structures that sequence two actions of the *same* subject inside a single clause, without needing a second conjugated verb at all.

## Avant de + infinitif: "before doing"

*Avant de* is followed directly by an infinitive, never a conjugated verb:

> *Avant de partir, j'ai fermé la porte.* — Before leaving, I closed the door.
>
> *Elle a réfléchi avant de répondre.* — She thought before answering.

The one hard requirement: both actions must share the same subject. "Before leaving" and "I closed the door" are both things *I* did. If the subjects differ — "before he arrived, I had already left" — *avant de* no longer works, and French switches to *avant que* followed by the subjunctive, a structure you'll meet properly in a later, more advanced unit. For now, the rule of thumb is simple: same subject, same clause, *avant de* + infinitive.

## Après avoir / après être + participe passé: "after doing"

The mirror structure for "after" needs a past participle instead of an infinitive, and — this is the part that surprises learners — it needs an auxiliary too, chosen exactly the way you'd choose it for the passé composé:

> *Après avoir mangé, elle est sortie.* — After eating, she went out.
>
> *Après être arrivée, elle a appelé sa mère.* — After arriving, she called her mother.

Most verbs use *avoir* here, since most verbs use *avoir* in the passé composé. The *être*-verbs (movement and state verbs like *arriver, partir, rentrer, sortir, naître, tomber*) use *être* instead — and crucially, the past participle still agrees with the subject, exactly as it would in an ordinary passé composé sentence:

> *Après être arrivés, ils ont posé leurs valises.* — After arriving, they put down their suitcases. (*arrivés*, plural, because *ils*)
>
> *Après s'être levée, elle a pris son café.* — After getting up, she had her coffee. (*levée*, feminine, reflexive with *être*)

## Why bother, when the plus-que-parfait already covers this ground

You could always say the same thing with two full clauses: *Elle avait mangé, puis elle est sortie* ("She had eaten, then she went out") uses the plus-que-parfait correctly and isn't wrong. But *avant de* and *après avoir/être* are tighter, more natural when both actions share a subject, and they're what fluent French speakers reach for constantly in both speech and writing. Overusing two full clauses with *puis* or *ensuite* for every sequence starts to sound like a children's book; these compact structures are what makes adult narration flow.

- *avant de* + infinitif — same subject required
- *après avoir* / *après être* + participe passé — auxiliary chosen like the passé composé, agreement still applies with être
- Both compress two actions of the same person into one clause
