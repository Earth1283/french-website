# Au moment où and the Signal Words That Hold a Story Together

Once you can jump backward with the plus-que-parfait and compress same-subject sequences with *avant de* / *après avoir*, one more tool pins two events to the *exact same instant*: *au moment où*.

## Au moment où: precise simultaneity

*Au moment où* means "just as" or "at the (exact) moment when" — it's more precise than a plain *quand*, which can leave some fuzziness about how closely two events line up:

> *Au moment où je suis sorti, il pleuvait.* — Just as I went out, it was raining.
>
> *Au moment où le train est arrivé, elle a couru vers le quai.* — Just as the train arrived, she ran toward the platform.

The typical pattern pairs the passé composé for the sudden event with the imparfait for the ongoing backdrop, the same imparfait/passé composé partnership you already use for description versus action. *Au moment où* simply narrows the focus to the precise instant where the two lines cross.

It also works to link two sudden events happening back-to-back:

> *Au moment où j'ai ouvert la porte, le chat s'est enfui.* — Just as I opened the door, the cat ran off.

## La veille: shifting "yesterday" inside a story

When you narrate something that happened in the past, words like *hier* (yesterday), *demain* (tomorrow), and *aujourd'hui* (today) stop making sense from the story's own point of view — you're not speaking from that day anymore. French has a set of shifted equivalents, and the most common one you'll need constantly is *la veille* (the day before):

> *Il était parti la veille.* — He had left the day before.

Compare: if you were speaking *on the day itself*, you'd say *il est parti hier*. But once you're narrating from a later point — telling the story today about something from last week — *hier* no longer anchors correctly, and *la veille* takes its place. (The forward equivalent, *le lendemain*, "the next day," works the same way and you'll meet it properly in a later unit.)

## Déjà and pas encore as timeline markers

Two more small words do a lot of sequencing work inside plus-que-parfait sentences, reinforcing what you saw in the first chapter of this lesson:

> *Quand nous sommes arrivés, le film avait déjà commencé.* — When we arrived, the film had already started.
>
> *Elle n'avait pas encore mangé quand nous sommes arrivés.* — She hadn't eaten yet when we arrived.

*Déjà* confirms the earlier event finished before the reference point; *ne ... pas encore* confirms it hadn't, at that point, started at all. Both are common enough that native speakers barely notice using them, but for a learner they're a reliable signal that the plus-que-parfait, not the passé composé, is the tense to reach for.

- *au moment où* = precise simultaneity, usually passé composé + imparfait
- *la veille* = "the day before," used when narrating in the past instead of *hier*
- *déjà* / *ne ... pas encore* = signal words that call for the plus-que-parfait
