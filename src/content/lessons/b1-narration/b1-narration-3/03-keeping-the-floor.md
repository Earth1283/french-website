# Keeping the Floor: Managing Your Own Story

Reactions from your listener keep a story alive, but the storyteller also needs tools to manage the flow: setting up the story, buying time, cutting a tangent short, and signaling that you're finally getting to the point. These small phrases are what separate a story that flows naturally from one that stalls every few seconds.

## Announcing you have a story: je te raconte

Before launching in, French speakers often flag that a story is coming, much like English "so, get this" or "let me tell you":

> *Je te raconte : hier, j'ai vu Marc à la gare.* — Let me tell you: yesterday, I saw Marc at the station.

*Je te raconte* (informal, *tu*) or *je vous raconte* (formal or plural) works as a verbal doorway into the anecdote — it tells your listener to switch into story-listening mode before you've said anything about the actual content yet.

## Buying time: attends

Mid-story, you'll often need a beat to remember a detail or find the right word. *Attends* ("wait," informal — *attendez* formally) does exactly what it does in English: it asks your listener to hold on a second without losing the floor:

> *Attends, comment il s'appelait déjà... Ah oui, Julien !* — Wait, what was his name again... Oh right, Julien!

This is a completely normal, frequent move in spoken storytelling. Fluent speakers pause and self-correct out loud constantly; trying to sound artificially smooth actually reads as less natural, not more.

## Cutting to the point: bref / enfin bref

Stories wander. When you catch yourself going down a tangent, or when you want to skip ahead to the important part, *bref* is the escape hatch:

> *Bon, il y avait beaucoup de monde, la queue était énorme, le serveur était en retard... bref, on est finalement arrivés à table vers 21h.* — Okay, there were a lot of people, the line was huge, the server was late... anyway, we finally got to the table around 9pm.

*Enfin bref* is the same move with a touch more emphasis — useful when the tangent ran especially long and you want to visibly signal "okay, back on track now." Both are extremely common in everyday spoken French and carry no rudeness; they're simply how French speakers self-edit out loud.

## Register: what's fine at dinner, not fine in an email

Every phrase in this lesson — *du coup*, *bref*, *je te raconte*, *attends* mid-sentence — belongs to spoken, informal register. They're exactly right at a dinner party, among friends, in a casual work chat. None of them belong in a formal email, a written report, or an oral exam answer at DELF B2 level, where you'd reach for more neutral connectors (*par conséquent*, *en résumé*, *pour conclure*) instead. Learning to code-switch between the lively spoken register and the neutral written one is itself a B1–B2 skill, not a minor detail.

- *je te raconte* — announces a story is coming
- *attends* — buys time mid-sentence without losing the floor
- *bref* / *enfin bref* — cuts a tangent short, gets back to the point
- All of the above: spoken register only, not for formal writing
