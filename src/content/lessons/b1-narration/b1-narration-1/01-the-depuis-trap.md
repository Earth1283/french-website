# The Depuis Trap: Why "I Have Lived" Stays in the Présent

English marks an action that started in the past and is still going on with the present perfect: "I have lived here for three years." French does something that feels backwards to an English brain — it uses the présent.

> *J'habite à Lyon depuis trois ans.* — I have been living in Lyon for three years.
>
> *Elle travaille ici depuis 2019.* — She has worked here since 2019.

The logic, once you see it, is simple: if the action is still true *right now*, French describes it with the tense for *right now*, which is the présent. The passé composé, by contrast, announces that something is finished. Say *j'ai habité à Lyon depuis trois ans* and you tell a French listener that you no longer live there — the opposite of what you meant.

## Depuis + noun vs depuis que + clause

*Depuis* works two ways. Before a noun or a duration, it stands alone:

> *Depuis la pandémie, il travaille de chez lui.* — Since the pandemic, he has worked from home.

Before a full clause with its own subject and verb, it needs *que*:

> *Depuis qu'il est parti, la maison est trop calme.* — Since he left, the house is too quiet.

Notice the event inside the *depuis que* clause (*il est parti*) is genuinely finished — it's the *result* (*la maison est trop calme*) that continues into the present. The présent rule applies to whichever half of the sentence describes the ongoing state, not necessarily every verb in the sentence.

## The spoken alternative: ça fait ... que

Conversational French often flips the structure around with *ça fait ... que*, putting the duration first:

> *Ça fait trois ans que j'habite à Lyon.* — It's been three years that I've been living in Lyon.

This means exactly the same thing as *j'habite à Lyon depuis trois ans* and follows the identical présent rule: the clause after *que* stays in the présent if the action is still ongoing. You will hear *ça fait ... que* constantly in casual conversation; treat it as depuis's natural spoken twin, not a separate grammar point.

## Why English speakers keep getting caught

The trap isn't the vocabulary — *depuis* is an easy word to learn. The trap is that your English instinct reaches for a past-oriented form ("I have lived") the moment you're describing something that started earlier. French asks you to ignore when the action *started* and focus only on whether it's *still true*. Before you say a *depuis* sentence out loud, ask yourself one question: is this still happening? If yes, the présent. If the action stopped, you don't need *depuis* at all — you need *pendant*, which the next chapter covers.

- *depuis* + noun/duration = since / for, action still ongoing, présent tense
- *depuis que* + clause = since (an event), same présent rule for the ongoing half
- *ça fait ... que* = spoken equivalent, same tense logic, duration fronted
