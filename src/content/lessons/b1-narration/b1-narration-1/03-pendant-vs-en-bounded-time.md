# Pendant vs En: Two Ways to Say "For" That Aren't Interchangeable

English "for" covers a lot of ground: "I lived there for two years," "I read the book for two hours," "I finished it in two hours." French splits these meanings across several words, and *pendant* and *en* are the two that trip up B1 learners most.

## Pendant: a closed span, finished or habitual

*Pendant* describes the duration *during which* something happened, without saying anything about whether that action is still going on. Pair it with the passé composé for a single completed period:

> *J'ai vécu à Berlin pendant deux ans.* — I lived in Berlin for two years.
>
> *Il a plu pendant toute la nuit.* — It rained all night long.

Both sentences describe a stretch that is over. Compare this to *depuis*, from the first chapter: *j'habite à Berlin depuis deux ans* means you *still* live there; *j'ai vécu à Berlin pendant deux ans* means that chapter is closed. Same two years, two completely different implications about the present.

*Pendant* can be dropped in casual speech when the meaning is clear from context (*j'ai attendu trois heures* works fine without it), but including it is never wrong and often clearer in writing.

## En: the time an action took to complete

*En* answers a different question entirely: not "during what span did this happen" but "how long did it take to get this action done." It measures the *speed* or *efficiency* of completing something:

> *J'ai lu ce roman en trois jours.* — I read this novel in three days.
>
> *Elle a appris l'espagnol en six mois.* — She learned Spanish in six months.

The action is bounded and complete either way, but *en* specifically highlights the time-to-completion, often implying something about how fast or slow that was. You could not swap *pendant* in here: *j'ai lu ce roman pendant trois jours* would mean you spent three days in the *process* of reading it (perhaps without finishing), while *en trois jours* means the whole novel was done within that window.

## A quick decision test

Ask what the sentence is really measuring:

- Measuring a stretch during which something occurred or continued → *pendant*
- Measuring how long it took to finish something → *en*
- Measuring a stretch still ongoing right now → *depuis* (présent tense)

> *Il a travaillé pendant huit heures et a fini le projet en six.* — He worked for eight hours and finished the project in six.

This sentence uses both in one breath: *pendant huit heures* is the total time he was working, *en six* is how long the project itself actually took — a perfectly natural, and very B1, way to narrate.

- *pendant* + duration = a finished or habitual span ("for," "during")
- *en* + duration = time taken to complete an action ("in")
- Both pair naturally with the passé composé, unlike *depuis*
