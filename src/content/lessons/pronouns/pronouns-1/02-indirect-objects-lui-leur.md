# Indirect Objects: lui, leur, and the Verbs That Trick You

English mostly stopped marking indirect objects with a preposition: "I told him" looks exactly like "I saw him." French did not stop. Whenever a French verb sends its action *to* someone — with an explicit or implied *à* — the pronoun changes shape entirely: not *le/la/les*, but *lui* or *leur*.

> *Je parle à mon frère.* → *Je lui parle.*
>
> *Elle écrit à ses amis.* → *Elle leur écrit.*

*Lui* covers "to him" and "to her" — one word, no gender. *Leur* covers "to them," singular in form even though the meaning is plural (don't be tempted to write "leurs" here; that *s* belongs to possessives, not pronouns).

## The verbs that catch English speakers off guard

A short list of everyday verbs takes *à* in French where the English translation feels direct:

- *téléphoner à quelqu'un* — to phone someone
- *demander à quelqu'un* — to ask someone
- *répondre à quelqu'un* — to answer someone
- *plaire à quelqu'un* — to please someone / to be liked by someone
- *ressembler à quelqu'un* — to resemble someone

Because English "call someone," "ask someone," and "answer someone" sound direct, learners reach for *le/la*. It's wrong every time. These verbs govern *à*, so their pronoun is always *lui* or *leur*:

> ~~Je le téléphone.~~ → **Je lui téléphone.**
>
> ~~Je la demande son numéro.~~ → **Je lui demande son numéro.**

There is no shortcut except memorizing which verbs behave this way — French dictionaries mark them with "qqn" preceded by "à." When you learn a new verb, check whether the object takes *à* before assuming it's direct.

## me, te, nous, vous: the pronouns that don't care

The first- and second-person object pronouns — *me, te, nous, vous* — do double duty as both direct and indirect objects. The same word works whether the verb takes *à* or not:

> *Tu me vois ?* (direct — *voir quelqu'un*)
>
> *Tu me réponds ?* (indirect — *répondre à quelqu'un*)

This is genuinely good news: you only need to learn one form per person, and *me/te* shorten to *m'/t'* before a vowel sound (*il m'aime*, *tu t'appelles*).

## Building the habit

Before you say a sentence with an object, run through: does the verb take *à*? If yes, it's *lui/leur* (or *me/te/nous/vous*, unaffected either way). If no, it's *le/la/les*. This two-question check becomes automatic with practice, but at this stage, slow down and ask it explicitly every time.
