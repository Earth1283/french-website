# Direct Objects: le, la, l', les

French does not repeat a noun it has already mentioned. Once "le film" is on the table, every later reference to it becomes a single tiny word: *le*. This is the core habit of this whole unit — French hides nouns in small words placed exactly where English speakers never think to look: **before** the verb.

> *Tu regardes le film ?* — *Oui, je le regarde.*
>
> *Tu connais ma sœur ?* — *Non, je ne la connais pas.*
>
> *Tu as pris les clés ?* — *Oui, je les ai prises.*

## Which one, and where does it go

- *le* replaces a masculine singular noun
- *la* replaces a feminine singular noun
- *l'* replaces either, before a vowel sound
- *les* replaces any plural

All four are **direct objects**: they replace a noun that receives the verb's action directly, with no preposition in between. *Regarder le film* — you watch the film, no "à," no "de." That noun becomes *le*.

The placement is the hard part for English speakers. In English, the pronoun goes after the verb: "I watch it." In French, it goes **before** the conjugated verb: *je le regarde*, never *je regarde le*. Say it out loud a few times — the muscle memory matters more than the rule.

## The subject/object test, early preview

Ask: is the noun *doing* the action, or *receiving* it? *Le film* is not watching anything — it is being watched. It receives the action, so it becomes a direct object pronoun. (You will meet this same test again with *qui* and *que* later in this unit — it is the same logic applied to relative clauses.)

## A trap: verbs that don't work this way

Not every French verb takes a direct object where English expects one. *Téléphoner à quelqu'un*, *demander à quelqu'un*, *répondre à quelqu'un* all use *à* — which means they take **indirect** object pronouns (*lui*, *leur*), covered in the next chapter. Mixing this up is one of the most common mistakes at this level: *"je le téléphone"* is wrong; it must be *je lui téléphone*.

## Negation squeezes the pronoun in, not out

The pronoun stays glued to the verb even inside a negative sentence. *Ne* moves to sit in front of the pronoun, and *pas* stays after the verb:

> *Je ne le regarde pas.* — I'm not watching it.
>
> *Je ne les connais pas.* — I don't know them.

Picture the whole block — *ne + pronoun + verb* — as one unbreakable unit, with *pas* stuck on the far side of it.

## Quick check

- *Tu manges la pomme ?* — *Oui, je ___ mange.* (la)
- *Tu vois mes parents ?* — *Non, je ne ___ vois pas.* (les)
- *Tu aimes ce livre ?* — *Oui, je ___ aime.* (l')

If those three came naturally, you have the core pattern. The rest of this lesson is about the words that behave differently — *lui*, *leur* — and about what happens to *le/la/les* when the sentence gets more complicated.
