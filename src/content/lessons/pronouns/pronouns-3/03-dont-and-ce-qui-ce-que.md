# Dont, and the Two Flavors of "What"

## Dont: for every verb that needs de

*Dont* replaces *de + noun*, the same job *en* does outside a relative clause. Any verb or expression built with *de* — *parler de, avoir besoin de, avoir envie de, être content de, se souvenir de* — hands its *de* over to *dont* when it becomes a relative clause:

> *Je parle de ce livre. Ce livre est excellent.* → *Le livre **dont** je parle est excellent.*
>
> *J'ai besoin de ce stylo.* → *Voici le stylo **dont** j'ai besoin.*
>
> *Elle est fière de son travail.* → *Le travail **dont** elle est fière.*

The trap: once you've used *dont*, the *de* is already accounted for — do not add a second one. *"Le livre dont je parle de"* is wrong; *dont* has already done that job.

*Dont* can also express possession ("whose"):

> *J'ai un collègue dont le fils est acteur.* — I have a colleague whose son is an actor.

## Ce qui / ce que: "what," with no noun to attach to

*Qui* and *que* always refer back to a specific noun already stated. When there is no such noun — you're talking about a whole idea, or something unspecified — French needs *ce qui* or *ce que*, literally "the thing which/that":

> *Dis-moi ce qui t'inquiète.* — Tell me what's worrying you. (subject: use ce qui)
>
> *Je ne comprends pas ce que tu veux dire.* — I don't understand what you mean. (object: use ce que)

The same subject/object test from the *qui/que* chapter still applies — only now it's wrapped in *ce* because there's no antecedent noun. *Ce qui t'inquiète* — the worrying thing is doing the worrying (of you); *ce que tu veux dire* — "tu" is the subject already, so the unnamed thing is the object.

There's also *ce dont*, for the same idea combined with a *de*-verb:

> *Ce dont j'ai besoin, c'est de temps.* — What I need is time.

## Quick check

- *Le film ___ tout le monde parle vient de sortir.* (dont)
- *Je ne sais pas ___ elle pense.* (ce qu' — before a vowel; "penser à" though would need a different structure — here assume "penser que")
- *___ m'intéresse, c'est l'histoire de France.* (Ce qui)

With *qui, que, où, dont,* and *ce qui/ce que* all in place, you have the full relative-pronoun toolkit. The last chapter puts them all to work in longer, natural sentences.
