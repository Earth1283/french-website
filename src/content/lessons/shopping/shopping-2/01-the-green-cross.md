# The Pharmacy Is Your First Stop

A French *pharmacie* is recognizable by its green cross, usually lit and sometimes blinking. Inside you will find medicine, first-aid supplies, cosmetics, and, most usefully, a trained pharmacist who can advise on minor complaints before you think about a doctor's appointment.

This is the anti-Duolingo approach in practice. You do not need perfect French to get help. You need a symptom, a clear request, and a willingness to answer questions.

## The phrases to start with

> *J'ai besoin de quelque chose contre le rhume.* — I need something for a cold.
>
> *J'ai mal à la tête depuis hier.* — I have had a headache since yesterday.
>
> *Avez-vous de l'aspirine ?* — Do you have any aspirin?

*J'ai besoin de* works for almost anything you cannot get by pointing. *Contre* means "against," which is why you can ask for something *contre la toux* (for a cough) or *contre le rhume* (for a cold). The grammar behind these phrases is simple, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

## What the pharmacist will ask

Expect a short interview. You may hear *Depuis quand ?* (Since when?), *Vous avez de la fièvre ?* (Do you have a fever?), or *Vous êtes allergique à quelque chose ?* (Are you allergic to anything?). Answer as directly as you can. A pharmacist recommending treatment needs facts, not a story.

## Prescription or not

Some medicines need *une ordonnance*, a prescription. Many common remedies do not, and the pharmacist can sell them directly. Ask *Ça se vend sans ordonnance ?* (Is this sold without a prescription?). If the answer is no, do not argue. The pharmacist knows the rules, and you do not.

## When the pharmacy is closed

Pharmacies take turns staying open at night and on Sundays. This arrangement is called the *pharmacie de garde*. Every pharmacy displays the address of the nearest one on duty, usually on the door. For a minor problem, a pharmacist is often the fastest first contact, which is exactly why the green cross deserves your attention.
