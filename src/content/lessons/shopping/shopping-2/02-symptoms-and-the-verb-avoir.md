# Build Symptoms With the Right Verb

Most symptom sentences in French use *avoir* (to have), not *être* (to be). English says "I am ill" or "I have a fever"; French says that you *have* pain or a fever. Learn each construction as a chunk, because the articles change with the body part.

## *Avoir mal à*: pain in a part of the body

*Avoir mal à* means "to have pain in." The form after *à* depends on the noun that follows:

| Body part | Phrase |
|---|---|
| *la tête* (head) | *J'ai mal à la tête.* |
| *le ventre* (stomach) | *J'ai mal au ventre.* |
| *les oreilles* (ears) | *J'ai mal aux oreilles.* |

*À la*, *au*, and *aux* are *à* combined with the article. Here *mal* is a noun meaning pain, not the adverb "badly." Learn the pattern with each new body part instead of memorizing isolated sentences.

## *Avoir de la fièvre*: the fever you have

*Fièvre* is feminine, so the partitive article is *de la*: *J'ai de la fièvre.* (I have a fever.) Avoid *J'ai une fièvre*, which is an English-shaped mistake. The same *de la* appears with other things you can have in quantity, which is why the phrase feels odd at first and then becomes automatic.

## *Besoin de*: asking for what you need

*J'ai besoin de* followed by a noun means "I need." Before a vowel, *de* becomes *d'*, so *J'ai besoin d'aspirine* is correct. No article is needed for a general request. If you mean a specific item, the article returns: *J'ai besoin du médicament* means "I need the medicine," the one the doctor mentioned.

## Pronunciation details

*Je* drops its vowel before *ai*, so you say *j'ai* as one smooth unit. In *Avez-vous*, the *-ez* ending is pronounced *ay*, and the *s* of *vous* stays silent. In *aspirine*, the final *e* is pronounced, and the stress falls quietly at the end of the word. Practice the whole phrase aloud, not just its parts.

Once you can say what hurts and what you need, the sentence structure is complete. The rest is recognizing the words a pharmacist uses in reply.
