# Four Traps English Speakers Fall Into

English-speaking learners make predictable mistakes at the pharmacy. None of them will stop you from being understood, but each one can make you sound confused or send you home with the wrong bottle.

## Trap one: *malade* versus *mal*

*Je suis malade* means "I am ill," a general statement about your condition. *J'ai mal* means "I am hurting," a statement about a specific pain. English uses "sick" for both, so you may reach for *Je suis mal* and sound like you are describing a bad situation rather than a symptom. Choose the form that matches what you feel.

## Trap two: *la grippe* versus *un rhume*

English "flu" is *la grippe*. A common cold is *un rhume*. Calling a cold *la grippe* is an exaggeration, and calling flu *un rhume* can lead the pharmacist to recommend the wrong treatment. If you are unsure, describe the symptoms and let the pharmacist choose.

## Trap three: the spelling of *aspirine*

The French word is *aspirine*, with an *e*. Writing *aspirin* on a phone screen or a sign may cause hesitation, and reading it aloud with an English accent can sound unfamiliar. Say it as *as-pi-REEN*, and the pharmacist will understand instantly.

## Trap four: confusing the shop, the medicine, and the person

*La pharmacie* is the shop. *Le médicament* is the medicine. *Le pharmacien* or *la pharmacienne* is the person behind the counter. Asking *Où est le médicament ?* gets you directions to the product, while *Où est la pharmacie ?* asks for the shop itself.

Notice the grammar in all four traps. The article tells you what you are talking about, so do not skip it. *Une ordonnance* is feminine, and *sans ordonnance* drops the article entirely because the phrase is a fixed expression. Learn the complete chunk rather than building it word by word.
