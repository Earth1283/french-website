# Traps English Speakers Fall Into in the Shop

Shopping mistakes are rarely dramatic. They are small errors that make the staff think you are a tourist who does not know the system, which is not the impression you want to give.

## *Je regarde* is not “I'm looking for”

English speakers say “I'm looking for a jacket” and then try *Je regarde une veste*. *Regarder* means to look at, watch or gaze. It describes what you are doing visually, not what you are seeking. Use *Je cherche une veste*.

## *Il est trop* versus *C'est trop*

For clothing, *C'est trop grand* or *C'est trop petit* is the natural sentence. *Il est trop grand* is also correct if you are referring to a masculine item already mentioned, but it can sound odd with *c'est* and a generic item. Learn the *c'est* form as a fixed phrase, and you will avoid the agreement puzzle.

## *Grand* and *gros* are not the same

For clothes, *grand* means too big in size, while *gros* means fat, heavy or large in a crude or rude sense. Never say *C'est trop gros* about a garment. Use *grand*.

## *Taille* versus *size* in English

French shops may use numbers, letters or both. The number 40 is not the same as an English size 40. Ask *Avez-vous une taille plus grande ?* (“Do you have a larger size?”) rather than guessing from the label.

## Gender with *veste*, *chemise* and *pantalon*

*Une veste* and *une chemise* are feminine. *Un pantalon* and *un manteau* are masculine. When you refer back to them, the pronoun must match: *Je la prends* for the jacket, *Je le prends* for the trousers. Mismatched pronouns are a common mistake for English speakers, and a small one in shops.

## Politely declining

*Ce n'est pas ce que je cherche* is a complete, polite refusal. You do not need to explain. If the staff member suggests something else, *Merci, je vais regarder encore* (“Thanks, I'll keep looking”) closes the exchange cleanly.
