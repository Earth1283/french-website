# What False Friends Are, and Why They Hurt

*Faux amis* ("false friends") are words that look like English words but mean something different. They exist because English and French share thousands of words from Latin, but over centuries the meanings drifted apart. Most shared words help you (*restaurant*, *important*, *possible*). A few mislead you, and those few cause the most embarrassing mistakes.

## The danger is confidence

A word you do not recognize makes you stop and check. A word that looks familiar makes you relax. That is exactly where a false friend gets you.

Three examples from this lesson:

| You see | You think | It really means |
|---|---|---|
| *préservatif* | preservative | condom |
| *blessé* | blessed | injured |
| *librairie* | library | bookshop |

*Préservatif* is the classic. A traveler asking a grocer for *des préservatifs* will cause surprise. In French, the word for a food preservative is *un conservateur*.

## Why they matter more in French

English borrowed a very large share of its vocabulary from French, which makes French read as familiar to English speakers. The overlap is a gift for reading, but a trap in speaking, because you have more false confidence.

## Two kinds of false friends

**Total false friends** have completely different meanings: *librairie* is a bookshop, not a library.

**Partial false friends** share one sense but not others: *actuellement* means "currently", never "actually." *Demander* looks like "demand" but just means "ask." To demand is *exiger*.

Partial false friends are harder to catch, because the word sometimes does match your English guess.

## A habit for new words

When a French word looks like English, stop and ask:

1. Does it make sense in this sentence?
2. Does it sound formal where the English word is casual (or vice versa)?
3. Is this a word that people say "oops" about?

If the answer to the last question is yes, check a dictionary.
