# Opinion Phrases That Skip the "Que"

Not every French opinion-starter behaves like a verb followed by a clause. A second family of expressions — *à mon avis*, *selon moi*, *pour moi* — works as a free-standing adverbial phrase. Mixing up the two families is one of the most common structural mistakes English speakers make at this level.

## No "que" needed

Because *à mon avis*, *selon moi*, and *pour moi* aren't verbs, they don't introduce a clause with *que*. They simply sit in front of, or inside, an ordinary complete sentence.

> *À mon avis, il vaut mieux partir tôt.* — In my opinion, it's better to leave early.
>
> *Selon moi, cette décision est risquée.* — In my view, this decision is risky.

Compare that to *je pense que*, which needs the *que* to link the opinion verb to its clause. Learners often bolt a *que* onto *à mon avis* by habit — *"à mon avis que..."* — which is simply wrong. Drop it.

## Three phrases, three registers

- **À mon avis** — the everyday default, safe in conversation and writing alike.
- **Selon moi** — a notch more formal. You'll hear it in debates, opinion columns, and any moment a speaker wants to sound considered rather than casual.
- **Pour moi** — informal and personal, closer to "to my mind" or "as I see it." It signals that what follows is your subjective take, not a claim about the world.

> *Pour moi, le plus important, c'est la santé.* — To my mind, the most important thing is health.

## Watch the double meaning of "pour moi"

The same three words, *pour moi*, also mean literally "for me" in ordinary contexts — ordering at a café, for instance.

> *Un café pour moi, s'il vous plaît.* — A coffee for me, please.

Position and context do the disambiguating. At the start of a sentence introducing a stance, it's an opinion marker. Attached to a noun or a request, it's just "for me."

## Where they go in the sentence

All three phrases are flexible: they usually open the sentence, but *selon moi* especially can also land mid-sentence for variety, particularly in writing:

> *Cette décision, selon moi, est risquée.*

That flexibility is one more reason these phrases don't behave like verbs — a verb has to stay put; an adverbial phrase can move.

## Quick contrast

> *Je pense que c'est risqué.* (verb + que + clause)
>
> *À mon avis, c'est risqué.* (adverbial phrase + independent clause)

Both sentences mean essentially the same thing. The grammar behind them does not. Getting this distinction automatic is what lets you build opinions fluently instead of pausing to recompute the structure every time.
