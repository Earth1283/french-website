# Refusing to Commit: Ça Dépend and D'un Côté... De l'Autre

Sometimes the most honest opinion is that there isn't a single answer. French treats this as a completely legitimate stance, not a dodge, and gives it its own set of dedicated phrases rather than forcing you to fake certainty.

## Ça dépend: the universal hedge

*Ça dépend* — "it depends" — is short, standalone, and immediately understood as a genuine answer, not an evasion.

> *— Tu préfères la ville ou la campagne ? — Ça dépend.*
>
> "Do you prefer the city or the countryside?" "It depends."

You can leave it bare, or extend it with *de* plus whatever it depends on:

> *Ça dépend du contexte.* — It depends on the context.
>
> *Ça dépend des gens.* — It depends on the people.

Note the preposition: *dépendre de*, so *ça dépend* always attaches its cause with *de*, contracted to *du* or *des* as needed — never *dépend sur*, a direct and incorrect calque of English "depend on."

## D'un côté... de l'autre: weighing two sides out loud

*D'un côté... de l'autre* — "on one hand... on the other" — is a paired connector. Unlike the single-word hedges, it requires both halves to make sense; starting with *d'un côté* creates an expectation that only *de l'autre* can resolve.

> *D'un côté, c'est cher ; de l'autre, c'est pratique.*
>
> On one hand it's expensive; on the other, it's practical.

You can drop the *un* and *l'autre* to sound slightly more literary — *d'un côté... d'un autre côté* is also heard — but the core pattern, one clause per side, stays the same. Leaving off the second half is a genuine structural error, not just an awkward stop: French listeners will wait for it.

## Combining both

These two phrases work well together, since *d'un côté... de l'autre* frequently sets up the very context that *ça dépend* points to:

> *Ça dépend. D'un côté, c'est plus rapide en voiture ; de l'autre, le train est plus écologique.*
>
> It depends. On one hand it's faster by car; on the other, the train is greener.

## Why this matters at B1

Below this level, learners tend to answer every opinion question with a flat *oui* or *non* because that's all the grammar they have. Being able to say "it depends, and here's why" is a genuine leap in expressive range — it lets you sound like someone who has actually thought about the question, which is exactly the impression a well-hedged opinion is supposed to give. The final chapter of this lesson adds phrases for summarizing that kind of weighed, two-sided thinking into a single closing statement.
