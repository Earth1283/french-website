# Asking for the Right Remedy

Once you have described your symptom, you need the names of the things you want. Most of the vocabulary here is short, and the grammar is one small rule about the word *contre*.

## The vocabulary you need

| French | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *la toux* | cough |
| *le rhume* | cold |
| *un médicament* | medicine |
| *des anti-douleurs* | painkillers |

*La toux* is pronounced /tu/. The final *x* is silent, so it sounds like "too" without the *t* sound at the end.

*Le rhume* is pronounced /ʁym/, close to "rhume" in the English word *rheum*, but with a rounder vowel. It is the ordinary word for a cold. Do not confuse it with *le rhume des foins*, which means hay fever.

## *Contre* takes the article

*Contre* means "against," and it takes whatever article the noun needs, with no contraction. So you say *contre la toux*, *contre le rhume*, and *contre les douleurs*. Never use *à* in this frame.

> *Je voudrais quelque chose contre le rhume.* — I would like something for a cold.
>
> *Vous avez un médicament contre la toux ?* — Do you have a cough medicine?

## Watch the false friend

*Un médicament* is a medicine. *Un médecin* is a doctor. English speakers sometimes swap them when they are stressed. If you say *je veux un médecin* at a pharmacy counter, you have asked for a person, not a product. Check the ending before you speak.

## Painkillers

*Des anti-douleurs* is the general term. If you want a specific brand, name it, but pharmacists often understand the active ingredient better than the brand name. Ask: *Quel anti-douleur me conseillez-vous ?* ("Which painkiller do you recommend?") This invites a recommendation rather than a yes or no.
