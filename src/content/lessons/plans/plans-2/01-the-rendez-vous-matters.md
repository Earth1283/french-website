# The Rendez-vous Matters More Than You Think

In France, a *rendez-vous* is a commitment. It can be a doctor's appointment, a meeting with a landlord, or a dinner with friends, and the same word covers all of them. Arriving on time and keeping to what you agreed signals respect, and a sudden cancellation is noticed.

## The word itself

*Un rendez-vous* is pronounced /œ̃ ʁɑ̃devu/. It comes from *se rendre*, to go to, and it is the imperative form that once meant "present yourselves." Today it is simply a fixed time and place. Its plural is *des rendez-vous*, with no change in the spelling of the word.

It is not only romantic. A *rendez-vous* with the bank, the tax office, or the dentist is just as ordinary. If you need to be somewhere at a set time, the word applies.

## Core phrases

> *Je suis libre à dix-huit heures.* — I'm free at six o'clock.
>
> *Je suis occupé(e) demain.* — I'm busy tomorrow.
>
> *On se retrouve où ?* — Where shall we meet?

*Libre* means free in the sense of not busy. It does not mean "free of charge," which is *gratuit*. Keep this distinction in mind when you see a poster advertising a free event.

## Adjective agreement

*Occupé* is the masculine form and *occupée* the feminine. Add *(e)* in your notes if you are unsure. The pronunciation is the same, but the spelling matters in a written message or on a calendar.

## Why it matters

Arriving late without warning is seen as careless in many settings. If you cannot make a time, say so early. A short message the day before is far better than a silent no-show, and it gives the other person room to adjust.
