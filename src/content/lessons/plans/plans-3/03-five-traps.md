# Five Traps in the Reservation Call

Most booking mistakes are not vocabulary failures. They come from translating English structure word for word. Watch for these.

## Trap 1: *je veux* for "I want"

The literal translation works, but it sounds blunt when you are asking a stranger for something. Use *je voudrais* by default and save *je veux* for friends and shopping lists.

## Trap 2: *réserver* is not "reserve" in every sense

*Réserver* means to book or hold something for a particular person. A *réserve* is a stock, a nature reserve or a shy restraint, and *réservé* describes a person who is private. For a table, the verb is *réserver*, and the noun is *une réservation*.

## Trap 3: *une pièce* is not a hotel room

*Une pièce* is a room in a flat or house, and it also means a single piece or a coin. In a hotel, ask for *une chambre*. If you say *une pièce*, the receptionist may ask whether you want an apartment.

## Trap 4: *Je m'appelle* on the phone

*Je m'appelle* introduces yourself in conversation. For a booking, *C'est au nom de* is the standard. Saying *Mon nom est* sounds like a formal introduction and makes the staff wait while you finish a speech they did not ask for.

## Trap 5: *complet* is not "complete"

*Complet* means full, as in fully booked. If you hear *Nous sommes complets*, the restaurant has no tables left. Ask *Avez-vous une table pour demain ?* or *Il n'y a rien de plus tôt ?* instead of repeating your original request.

The same logic applies to rooms. If the hotel is full, ask *Avez-vous une chambre pour une autre nuit ?* — Do you have a room for another night? Offering flexibility often gets you a yes faster than insisting on the original plan.
