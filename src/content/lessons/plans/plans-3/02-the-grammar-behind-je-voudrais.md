# The Grammar Behind *Je voudrais*

The booking script is short, so it is easy to memorise as a block. Understanding its grammar lets you adapt it when the number of guests, the date or the hotel details change.

## Why *voudrais* is polite

*Voudrais* is the conditional of *vouloir*, "to want." The conditional softens a request, much as English uses "would" in "I would like." Compare:

- *Je veux réserver.* — I want to book. Grammatically fine, but it sounds like a demand.
- *Je voudrais réserver.* — I would like to book. The default polite form.

Other forms you may need are *tu voudrais*, *vous voudriez* and *nous voudrions*. The ending of *voudrais* is pronounced like the *è* in *père*, and the final *s* is silent.

## One verb after another

*Je voudrais* is followed directly by an infinitive: *réserver*, *annuler*, *confirmer*. No *à* or *de* is needed. Think of the frame as *je voudrais + verb + object*.

## *Pour* with numbers

*Une table pour deux* uses *pour* plus a number to mean "for two." The same pattern works for other bookings: *une chambre pour trois personnes*. Nothing is inserted between *pour* and the number.

## *Combien de* and the plural

*Pour combien de personnes ?* uses *combien de* followed by a plural noun, so *personnes* stays plural. Your answer usually repeats only the number: *Pour deux.* If the staff member asks a yes-or-no question about the group, you can reply with *Nous sommes trois.* — There are three of us.

## The name idiom

*C'est au nom de* is a fixed idiom. *Au* is the contraction of *à* and *le*, and the surname follows without an article: *au nom de Dupont*, not *au nom du Dupont*. The *e* of *ce* drops before *est*, which is why it is written *c'est*.
