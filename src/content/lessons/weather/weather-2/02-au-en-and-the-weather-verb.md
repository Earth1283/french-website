# *Au* or *En*, and the Verb *Faire* for Weather

The grammar behind seasons is a single choice: *au* or *en*. Once you understand the pattern, you can say *in spring*, *in summer*, *in autumn* and *in winter* without guessing.

## *Au printemps*

*Printemps* is masculine and begins with a consonant. French uses *au* (a contraction of *à* and *le*) with masculine nouns that start with a consonant. So you say *au printemps*, pronounced [o pʁɛ̃tɑ̃].

## *En été, en automne, en hiver*

The other three seasons take *en*. *En* is used with seasons that begin with a vowel sound, and *été*, *automne* and *hiver* all do. Because *en* does not combine with the article, the sentence stays simple: *en été*, *en automne*, *en hiver*.

Liaison matters in *en hiver*. Say [ɑ̃‿ivɛʁ], linking the *n* to the vowel. Without the liaison, the phrase sounds slightly clipped.

## Weather verbs

Weather in French often uses *faire*, the verb "to do" or "to make." You do not say *the weather is*; you say *il fait*.

> *Il fait chaud.* — It's hot.
>
> *Il fait froid.* — It's cold.
>
> *Il fait beau.* — The weather is fine.

*Il fait* is the core pattern. You can attach a temperature to it, or a quality such as *beau* or *mauvais*, and the sentence works.

## Asking for the temperature

> *Il fait combien de degrés ?* — What's the temperature?

*Combien de* asks "how many" for a countable quantity. *Degrés* is plural, so it follows the word *de*. The answer does not need *de*: *Il fait vingt-cinq degrés.* ("It's twenty-five degrees.")

France uses Celsius, so a sentence like *Il fait trente-deux degrés* is a serious statement. When you hear the word *canicule*, the temperature is likely high enough for the news to mention it.

## A quick check

If you are unsure, say the season with *au* or *en* and ask about the temperature. *Au printemps, il fait combien de degrés ?* is grammatical, and it lets you practise the preposition and the question in one sentence.
