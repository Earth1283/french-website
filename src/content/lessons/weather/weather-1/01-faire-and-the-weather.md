# *Faire* and the Weather

The most common French weather expressions use the verb *faire* ("to make" or "to do"). Not *être*, not *avoir*. Once you accept that the weather "makes" itself, the rest follows a single pattern.

## The pattern: *il fait* + adjective

The subject is always *il* (it), with no real meaning: it is an impersonal subject.

| French | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *Il fait beau.* | The weather is nice. |
| *Il fait mauvais.* | The weather is bad. |
| *Il fait chaud.* | It's hot. |
| *Il fait froid.* | It's cold. |
| *Il fait frais.* | It's cool. |
| *Il fait doux.* | It's mild. |
| *Il fait lourd.* | It's muggy. |
| *Il fait sombre.* | It's dark. |

There is no article, no adjective agreement, and no tense changes to learn. Just *il fait* + the word.

## A common mistake

English says "it is hot." Beginners write *Il est chaud*. That sentence describes a hot object, such as soup or a plate. For weather, **always** use *faire*:

- ✔ *Il fait chaud aujourd'hui.*
- ✘ *Il est chaud aujourd'hui.*

Likewise, *Il est beau* means "he is handsome." For the weather it is *Il fait beau*.

## When *you* are hot or cold

For your own sensation, French uses *avoir* (to have):

> *J'ai chaud.* — I'm hot.
>
> *J'ai froid.* — I'm cold.
>
> *Tu as chaud ?* — Are you hot?

This is the same pattern as *J'ai faim* and *J'ai soif*. Saying *Je suis chaud* is a mistake with a different meaning in some contexts, and best avoided.

So there are two systems that never mix:

| Situation | Verb |
|---|---|
| The weather | *Il fait chaud* |
| Your body | *J'ai chaud* |

## Weather with nouns: *du, de la*

Some expressions use *faire* with a noun, sometimes with a partitive article:

- *Il fait soleil.* — It's sunny (no article).
- *Il fait du vent.* — It's windy.
- *Il fait du brouillard.* — It's foggy.

They overlap with *il y a*, which comes next.

## Temperature

Add the number and *degrés*:

> *Il fait vingt degrés.* — It's 20 degrees.
>
> *Il fait moins cinq.* — It's minus five.

France uses Celsius.
