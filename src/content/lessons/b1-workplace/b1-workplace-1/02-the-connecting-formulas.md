# The Connecting Formulas That Hold an Email Together

Once you're past the greeting, French professional email leans on a small set of fixed chunks to do the structural work — linking to a previous exchange, softening a request, pointing at an attachment. English speakers often try to build these sentences word by word from scratch, which produces something grammatically fine but stylistically foreign. Learn the chunks instead.

## Suite à... — picking up a thread

*Suite à* means "following" or "further to," and it opens an email that continues something already in motion: a call, a meeting, a previous message.

> *Suite à notre réunion de mardi, voici le compte-rendu.*
> — Following our meeting on Tuesday, here is the summary.

It's always followed directly by a noun phrase, never a full clause. You cannot say *"suite à nous avons parlé"* — you'd need *suite à notre conversation*.

## Je me permets de... — asking permission to bother someone

*Je me permets de* literally means "I allow myself to," and it prefaces an action you're taking that might otherwise feel presumptuous: writing unprompted, following up, asking for something.

> *Je me permets de vous relancer au sujet du dossier.*
> — I'm taking the liberty of following up on the file.

It sounds almost apologetic in English, but in French it's simply standard professional courtesy — a small bow before you ask for someone's time.

## Vous trouverez ci-joint... — pointing at an attachment

*Ci-joint* means "attached herewith." The safest, most common form keeps it invariable after *vous trouverez*:

> *Vous trouverez ci-joint le devis demandé.*
> — Please find attached the requested quote.

Strict formal style sometimes agrees *ci-joint* with the noun that follows (*ci-jointe la facture*), but nobody will fault you for leaving it as shown above — it's what native speakers write by far the most often.

## N'hésitez pas à... — inviting a reply

*N'hésitez pas à* + infinitive is the standard way to close off a paragraph by inviting further contact:

> *N'hésitez pas à me contacter pour toute question.*
> — Don't hesitate to contact me with any questions.

Note the preposition: *n'hésitez pas à*, never *n'hésitez pas de*. English "hesitate to" maps directly onto *à*, which is one of the rare cases where the two languages agree.

## Why chunks, not word-by-word translation

Each of these phrases has a fixed shape: a set preposition, a set position in the sentence, a set level of formality. Translating "I'm taking the liberty of" freshly every time will eventually produce something a French reader has to decode; using *je me permets de* lets them read straight through. That fluency is the entire point of learning formulas as blocks rather than as individual words.
