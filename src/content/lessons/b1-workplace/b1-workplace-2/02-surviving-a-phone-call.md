# Surviving a French Phone Call

A phone call strips out everything that makes face-to-face French easier — gesture, facial expression, the chance to point at something. What's left is pure formula, which is actually good news: a handful of fixed phrases cover almost every phone situation you'll meet at work.

## Transferring a call: je vous passe... / ne quittez pas

When a call needs to go to someone else, the standard sequence is:

> *Je vous passe le service comptabilité, ne quittez pas.*
> — I'll put you through to the accounting department, please hold.

*Ne quittez pas* literally means "don't leave" — it's the fixed phrase for "please hold," and it's worth learning exactly as it is rather than improvising with *attendez*, which sounds closer to a command than a courtesy.

## Asking for repetition: pourriez-vous répéter ?

This is where the conditional politeness you've already met elsewhere earns its keep. *Pourriez-vous* is the conditional of *pouvoir*, and on the phone — where a bad line, an accent, or fast speech can genuinely defeat you — it does more work than it would in person, because your tone and apologetic face aren't there to soften a blunter *"répétez !"*

> *Pourriez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ? La ligne est mauvaise.*
> — Could you repeat that, please? The line is bad.

The same conditional softening shows up throughout professional phone French: *pourriez-vous patienter*, *pourriez-vous m'épeler votre nom*. Once you have *pourriez-vous* + infinitive solid, most phone requests fall into place automatically.

## Promising a callback: je vous rappelle

*Rappeler* means "to call back" — a straightforward, transparent verb, as long as you don't confuse it with the reflexive *se rappeler*, "to remember." They share a root but do entirely different jobs.

> *Je vous rappelle dans dix minutes, je suis en réunion.*
> — I'll call you back in ten minutes, I'm in a meeting.

## Closing the loop: on se tient au courant

At the end of a call where nothing is fully resolved yet, *on se tient au courant* signals that both sides will follow up as things develop — a soft, open-ended close rather than a hard end to the conversation.

> *Parfait, on se tient au courant. Bonne journée !*
> — Great, we'll keep each other posted. Have a good day!

## Why the phone is harder, and why it's still learnable

Because you can't see the other person, French phone etiquette compensates with slightly more explicit verbal signposting than a face-to-face chat needs — you announce transfers, you announce holds, you announce callbacks. Learn the four phrases above as fixed units, and a huge share of routine office calls becomes close to scripted.
