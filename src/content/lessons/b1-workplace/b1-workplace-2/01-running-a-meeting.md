# Running a Meeting Without Losing the Thread

A French *réunion* has its own small vocabulary, and most of it is designed to keep a group of people moving through an agenda without anyone quietly losing track of what's being decided. Learn these chunks and a meeting stops sounding like background noise.

## L'ordre du jour — the agenda

*L'ordre du jour* is literally "the order of the day," and it has nothing to do with mood or weather — it's the fixed list of points a meeting will cover, almost always sent or read out at the start.

> *Voici l'ordre du jour de la réunion : le budget, le planning, puis les questions diverses.*
> — Here's the agenda for the meeting: the budget, the schedule, then any other business.

If a meeting seems to be drifting, *"On reste sur l'ordre du jour ?"* ("Are we sticking to the agenda?") is a polite way to pull it back on track.

## Faire le point — taking stock

*Faire le point* means to pause and assess where things stand — on a project, a budget, a negotiation. It's one of the most common phrases in French office life, precisely because so many meetings exist for no other reason than to do this.

> *On va faire le point sur le projet avant la fin de la semaine.*
> — We're going to review where the project stands before the end of the week.

Resist translating this literally as "make the point" — it isn't about making an argument, it's about taking a status snapshot.

## Prendre la parole — taking the floor

*La parole* is "speech" as an abstract thing — the right or turn to talk — and you *take* it rather than *have* it, which is the opposite of how English usually frames speaking turns.

> *Je peux prendre la parole avant qu'on passe au point suivant ?*
> — Can I take the floor before we move to the next point?

It's the standard way to signal you want to speak in a meeting with any degree of structure, more formal than simply starting to talk.

## Une réunion is not always optional

French office culture treats meetings as a normal, expected part of the week rather than an interruption to "real work" — so the vocabulary above gets heavy daily use. Being fluent in it matters more than it might first appear: a colleague who can navigate *l'ordre du jour*, *faire le point*, and *prendre la parole* smoothly reads as someone who belongs in the room, independent of how advanced their grammar is elsewhere.

## Quick reference

- *l'ordre du jour* — the agenda, read at the start
- *faire le point* — to review where things stand
- *prendre la parole* — to take a speaking turn
- *"On reste sur l'ordre du jour ?"* — a polite way to redirect a drifting meeting
