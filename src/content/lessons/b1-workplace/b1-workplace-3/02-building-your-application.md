# Building Your Application

Once you know the vocabulary of a job posting, the next step is describing yourself in it — the documents you send, and the two phrases nearly every French interview eventually calls for.

## Une lettre de motivation

Almost every French application expects a *lettre de motivation* — a cover letter — alongside the CV, even for roles where English-speaking countries might skip it entirely. It exists to explain, in your own words, why this specific position and this specific company, rather than simply restating what the CV already lists.

> *Chaque candidature demande une lettre de motivation.*
> — Every application requires a cover letter.

Skipping it, or sending a generic one that could apply to any job, tends to read as a lack of real interest — French recruiters generally expect it to be tailored each time.

## Points forts et points faibles

The strengths-and-weaknesses question shows up in French interviews just as reliably as it does in English ones, and the vocabulary is straightforward:

> *Quels sont vos points forts et vos points faibles ?*
> — What are your strengths and weaknesses?

The trick, as in English, isn't the vocabulary — it's the answer. A *point faible* framed as a real weakness with no context can sound careless; framed as something you're actively working on, it reads as self-aware instead.

## Être motivé / motivée

Saying you're motivated for a role is close to expected in a French interview, and the phrase is simple — but it comes with a grammar detail that's easy to get wrong under pressure.

> *Je suis motivé pour ce poste.* (a man speaking)
> *Je suis motivée pour ce poste.* (a woman speaking)

The adjective agrees with the speaker's gender, the way most French adjectives after *être* do. Job postings write *motivé(e)* with the parenthetical *e* to cover both readers at once — but that's a convention for written notices addressing an unknown audience, not something you'd ever say aloud or write about yourself. Speaking or writing about yourself, pick the one form that matches you.

## Putting the pieces together

A short, realistic self-introduction combining these pieces might run:

> *Je suis motivée pour ce poste parce qu'il correspond exactement à mon profil. Mon point fort, c'est l'organisation ; mon point faible, c'est que j'ai parfois du mal à déléguer.*
> — I'm motivated for this position because it matches my profile exactly. My strength is organization; my weakness is that I sometimes struggle to delegate.

Notice the structure doing the real work: a reason for the motivation, one clear strength, one honest but manageable weakness. That shape, more than any single word, is what a French interviewer is actually listening for.
