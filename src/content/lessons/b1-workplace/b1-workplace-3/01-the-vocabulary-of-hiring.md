# The Vocabulary of Hiring

Job hunting in France comes with its own small dictionary, and two of the most important words look almost nothing like their English counterparts — which is exactly why they're worth learning properly rather than guessing.

## Un poste and une candidature

*Un poste* is a position or a job opening — the thing being advertised.

> *Le poste est toujours disponible ?*
> — Is the position still available?

*Une candidature* is your application — the package of documents and the act of applying.

> *J'ai envoyé ma candidature hier.*
> — I sent my application yesterday.

Here's the trap: *une application* exists in French, but it means a phone app, nothing else. Reaching for it to mean "job application" is one of the most common false-friend errors English speakers make in this context. The verb you want is *postuler* ("to apply"), and the noun is *une candidature*.

## Un entretien (d'embauche)

*Un entretien* on its own just means "an interview" or even "a conversation" in a broader sense; adding *d'embauche* ("of hiring") makes clear you mean a job interview specifically.

> *J'ai un entretien d'embauche demain matin.*
> — I have a job interview tomorrow morning.

In context, *un entretien* alone is usually enough — French speakers rarely mistake it for anything else once you're talking about jobs.

## Un stage

*Un stage* is an internship — a fixed-term, often unpaid or lightly paid placement, common for students and young professionals. The trap here runs the other way: English "stage," as in a theatrical stage or a phase of a project, has no connection to this word at all. Reading a job posting and seeing *stage* does not mean the theater — it means an internship.

## Un CDI vs un CDD

This pair matters more in France than the English "permanent vs. fixed-term contract" distinction might suggest.

- *Un CDI* (*contrat à durée indéterminée*) — a permanent, open-ended contract. The genuinely prized outcome of a job search.
- *Un CDD* (*contrat à durée déterminée*) — a fixed-term contract, with a set end date.

> *C'est un CDI ou un CDD ?*
> — Is it a permanent contract or a fixed-term one?

Because French labor law makes permanent contracts comparatively hard to end, a CDI carries real weight beyond the job itself — banks, landlords, and lenders often treat it as a sign of financial stability, sometimes asking for proof of one before approving a loan or a lease.

## Quick reference

- *un poste* — a position
- *une candidature* — an application (never *une application*)
- *un entretien (d'embauche)* — a job interview
- *un stage* — an internship, not a theatrical stage
- *un CDI / un CDD* — permanent / fixed-term contract
