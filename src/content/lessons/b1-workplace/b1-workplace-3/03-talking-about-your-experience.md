# Talking About Your Experience: depuis vs. pendant

This is the single grammar point most likely to trip up an English speaker in a French job interview, and it comes up constantly, because "how long have you done X" is one of the first questions any interviewer asks.

## The English instinct, and why it misleads you

In English, "I have worked here for three years" uses the present perfect — and the natural instinct is to reach for the closest French equivalent, the passé composé: *"j'ai travaillé ici pendant trois ans."* That sentence is grammatical French. The problem is what it means: it says you worked there for three years, and you don't anymore. It describes a finished chapter, not your current job.

## Still true now: present tense + depuis

If the situation is still ongoing — you're describing your current job in a current interview — French uses the present tense, not the passé composé, paired with *depuis*:

> *Je travaille ici depuis trois ans.*
> — I've been working here for three years (and I still do).

This is genuinely one of the biggest structural differences between the two languages' ways of talking about time: English marks "started in the past, continues now" with a perfect tense; French marks the exact same idea with a present tense plus *depuis*. Neither is more "logical" than the other — they're just different systems, and importing the English logic into French produces a sentence that quietly says the opposite of what you meant.

## A closed chapter: passé composé + pendant

*Pendant* pairs naturally with the passé composé precisely because it marks a bounded, completed stretch of time:

> *J'ai travaillé chez Orange pendant deux ans.*
> — I worked at Orange for two years.

Here the job is over — you moved on, and *pendant* plus the passé composé together mark that clearly.

## The two side by side

> *Je travaille ici depuis trois ans, mais j'ai travaillé chez Orange pendant deux ans.*
> — I've been working here for three years, but I worked at Orange for two years.

One clause, present job, still ongoing, *depuis*. The other clause, past job, finished, passé composé plus *pendant*. Swapping the two — passé composé for the current job, or *depuis* with a job you've left — is exactly the kind of small error that doesn't block understanding but does mark you instantly as translating from English rather than thinking in French.

## The test to run in your head

Before choosing, ask: is it still true right now? If yes, present tense + *depuis*. If no, passé composé + *pendant*. That single question resolves the vast majority of cases you'll meet in an interview, on a CV, or in casual conversation about your career.
