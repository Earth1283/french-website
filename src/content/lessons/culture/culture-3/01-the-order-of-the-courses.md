# The Order of the Courses

A French dinner has a shape, and knowing the shape tells you what is coming next. It is a sequence of courses, each with its own name. The names are not hard. The surprises are in the order and in one word that means something unexpected.

## The traditional sequence

A full meal typically runs in this order:

1. ***L'entrée*** — the starter
2. ***Le plat principal*** — the main course
3. ***Le fromage*** — the cheese course
4. ***Le dessert*** — the dessert

Not every meal includes all four. A lunch in a café may offer only a main course, and a dinner at home may skip the starter. But when the full meal is served, this is the order most people expect.

## The starter is *l'entrée*

The most important word here is *l'entrée*, which means the starter, not the main dish. This is the reverse of how many English speakers use the word, and it is the source of countless confused orders. If you want the main course, ask for ***le plat*** or ***le plat principal***.

## Why cheese comes before dessert

Many English speakers are surprised that *le fromage* comes before *le dessert*. In traditional French meals, the cheese course is a transition from savoury to sweet. It is a customary order rather than a legal requirement, but it is widely followed in homes and restaurants, and you will rarely see it reversed.

## The after-dinner drink

After dessert, or sometimes after the cheese, some people order ***un digestif***. It is usually a strong spirit served in a small glass, such as a cognac or a calvados. The word means something that aids digestion, and the custom is more about ritual than medicine. You can decline politely with *Non merci, pour moi c'est assez*, which means “No thank you, that is enough for me.”

Learn the four courses and their order. Once you know the sequence, the menu stops being a list of unknown words and becomes a plan for the evening.
