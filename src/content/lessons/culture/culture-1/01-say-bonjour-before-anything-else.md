# Say Bonjour Before Anything Else

In France, a conversation begins with a greeting, not a request. Walk into a boulangerie and ask for bread without saying hello, and you have not been efficient; you have been rude. The sale is fine, but the shopkeeper will remember the tone. This lesson covers the phrases that make the first thirty seconds work.

## The greeting is part of the transaction

*Bonjour* is the default greeting from morning until the evening. After about six o'clock, many people switch to *Bonsoir*. Say it when you enter a shop, a café, or a waiting room, and say it to the person you are speaking to, not just into the air.

> *Bonjour, Madame.* — Good morning, madam.
>
> *Bonsoir, Monsieur.* — Good evening, sir.

Adding *Madame* or *Monsieur* is a sign of respect. Do not add a surname unless you know it.

## Getting attention politely

If you need someone's attention, begin with ***Excusez-moi***, which means “excuse me.” It is more polite than the word *pardon* alone, which is what you say after a small bump in a crowded place. *Excusez-moi* is also how you start a question: *Excusez-moi, où est la gare ?*

## Asking for things

Every request ends with ***s'il vous plaît***. It is used constantly, including to get a waiter's attention. The phrase does real work. It turns a demand into a courteous request, and it is the expected finish to nearly every interaction in a shop or restaurant.

> *Un croissant, s'il vous plaît.* — A croissant, please.

## Meeting someone new

When you are introduced to someone, the standard response is ***Enchanté(e)***, meaning delighted to meet you. Men say *enchanté*; women say *enchantée*. Both are pronounced the same, so the difference only appears in writing, but the spelling still matters.

## Why this matters

The French word for this social habit is *la politesse*, and it is less about formality than about acknowledging the other person before you ask for anything. A brief greeting costs nothing and changes how the rest of the exchange sounds. Start every encounter with a greeting, and most of the courtesy follows naturally.
