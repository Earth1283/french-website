# The Courtesy Traps

Most politeness mistakes are not dramatic. They are small habits carried over from English, which sound harmless to you and slightly off to a French listener. Here are the five most common ones, with a better alternative for each.

## Trap 1: shouting for the waiter

Calling *garçon!* across a café, snapping your fingers, or waving a card in the air are all signs of impatience. Use *s'il vous plaît* and make eye contact. A waiter who is busy will usually look over within a few seconds.

## Trap 2: using *tu* too early

English has one word for “you,” so the switch to *tu* can feel like a small thing. In French it is a decision about closeness. Using *tu* with an older stranger is a common mistake, and it sounds too familiar. If you do it by accident, correct yourself gracefully (see the final section of the next chapter).

## Trap 3: *Pardon* and *Excusez-moi* mixed up

*Pardon* is a quick apology for a minor collision. *Excusez-moi* is the phrase for getting attention or asking to pass. You can say *pardon* after bumping someone on the metro, but start a question with *excusez-moi*.

## Trap 4: an excessive greeting

The bise is a light kiss on the cheek, not a hug. In most social settings, people kiss cheeks with friends and acquaintances. With new people, or in a professional setting, a handshake is common. The safe rule is to let the other person lead. If they offer a hand, take it. If they lean in, lean in too.

## Trap 5: *sympathique* as “sympathetic”

*Sympathique* means nice or pleasant, not sympathetic in the English sense of feeling sorry for someone. If you want to say a person is kind, *gentil* works well: *Elle est très gentille.* (“She is very kind.”) The trap is the false friend, not the grammar.

Politeness in French is less about a list of rules and more about attention. The errors come from speed. Slow down, greet first, and choose the register that fits.
