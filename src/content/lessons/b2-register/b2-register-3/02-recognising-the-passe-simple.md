# Recognising the Passé Simple (and Its Subjunctive Cousin)

Open a French novel, a history textbook, or a formal news feature, and you'll meet verb forms that never come up in conversation. The most important is the **passé simple**, a literary past tense. This chapter has one job: teach you to *recognise* it confidently. You will not be asked to produce it — French speakers themselves rarely write it outside formal narration, and never speak it.

## What it looks like

The passé simple tells a story from the outside, the way "and then he said" works in an English novel, as opposed to conversational "and then he was like." Two irregular forms show up constantly enough to memorise on sight:

> *être:* il fut, elle fut, ils furent — "he/she was, they were"
>
> *avoir:* il eut, elle eut, ils eurent — "he/she had, they had"

> *Il fut le premier à comprendre la situation.* — "He was the first to understand the situation."
>
> *Elle eut un moment d'hésitation avant de répondre.* — "She had a moment's hesitation before answering."

The key trap: *fut* is not a typo of *fût*, and *eut* is not random letters — both are ordinary conjugated forms of extremely common verbs, just in a tense you haven't drilled for production. Once you know to expect them in narration, they stop looking like errors and start looking like normal storytelling French.

Other verbs follow visible patterns too: *-er* verbs take *-a/-èrent* (*il alla*, *ils allèrent* — irregular stem, regular passé simple ending pattern for aller), *-ir/-re* verbs often take *-it/-irent*.

## The imparfait du subjonctif: reading-only

Even rarer, and now essentially extinct outside 19th-century literature and very formal legal or literary registers, is the **imparfait du subjonctif** — a subjunctive mood built on the passé simple stem. It shows up after a past-tense main clause where modern French would just use the ordinary present subjunctive.

> *Bien qu'il fût tard, elle continua à lire.* — "Although it was late, she kept reading."
>
> Modern equivalent: *Bien qu'il soit tard, elle continuait à lire.*

The telltale sign is the circumflex: *fût* (with accent) is imparfait du subjonctif, *fut* (no accent) is passé simple indicative. They're pronounced identically and mean subtly different things, but you will only ever need to recognise them, never produce them — even highly educated native speakers rarely use this mood actively today.

## Why bother at all

You'll meet these forms in novels, in some journalism, in historical texts, and occasionally in very formal ceremonial speech (wedding toasts, official commemorations). Recognising *fut/eut/allèrent* as ordinary past-tense storytelling, rather than mysterious archaic grammar, removes a real barrier to reading anything above a functional level — news articles aside, most French literature you'll want to read eventually uses this tense throughout.
