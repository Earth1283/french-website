# Writing in Formal French

You've learned how the same idea shifts between casual and neutral registers, and how spoken French strips grammar down to its bare essentials. This lesson goes the other direction: registre soutenu, the formal French of cover letters, official emails, administrative forms, and literature. You won't use most of this in daily conversation — but you will need to read it, and there are real situations (job applications, formal complaints, addressing someone's parents for the first time) where you'll need to produce it.

## Inversion: the formal question mark

Everyday spoken French asks questions with intonation or "est-ce que": *Tu viens ?* / *Est-ce que tu viens ?* Formal French instead inverts the subject and verb, joined with a hyphen:

> *Viendrez-vous à la réunion ?* — "Will you be coming to the meeting?"
>
> *Pourriez-vous me confirmer votre présence ?* — "Could you confirm your attendance?"

Inversion appears constantly in written formal questions and almost never in casual speech — hearing someone invert a question out loud in a bar would sound like a costume drama.

## The formal "nous"

Where spoken French replaces "nous" with "on," formal writing does the opposite: it leans on "nous" heavily, often as an institutional or collective voice, even when a single person is writing.

> *Nous vous prions de bien vouloir patienter.* — "We kindly ask you to please wait."
>
> *Nous avons bien reçu votre dossier.* — "We have received your file."

This "we" isn't necessarily literal — a single administrator writing on behalf of an office will still use "nous." It's a formality convention, similar to the English institutional "we regret to inform you."

## Formal doesn't mean flowery for its own sake

A common mistake is assuming "more formal" means "more words" or "fancier vocabulary" indiscriminately. Good formal French is precise and structured, not padded. The goal of the next two chapters is to give you the specific building blocks — recognising literary past tenses when you meet them in reading, using the right connectors, and avoiding the anglicisms that quietly mark a piece of writing as "translated" rather than "written in French."

> *Il s'agit d'un désaccord entre les deux parties.* — "This concerns a disagreement between the two parties."

Notice this sentence isn't ornate — it's just correctly, cleanly formal. That's the real target: not sounding impressive, but sounding like you belong in the register you're writing in.
