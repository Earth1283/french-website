# Formal Connectors and Anglicism Traps

Two things separate competent formal French from formal French that quietly reads as "written by someone translating from English": the connectors that link ideas, and a handful of specific anglicism traps that even confident learners fall into.

## Formal connectors

Courant French links contrasting ideas with "mais" (but). Soutenu French reaches for something with more weight:

> *Le projet est ambitieux ; néanmoins, le budget reste limité.* — "The project is ambitious; however, the budget remains limited."
>
> *Toutefois, cette solution présente un inconvénient majeur.* — "However, this solution has one major drawback."
>
> *Cependant, il convient de rester prudent.* — "However, caution is still warranted."

*Néanmoins*, *toutefois*, and *cependant* are close to interchangeable and all signal "however" at a formal register — using one instead of "mais" is one of the fastest ways to lift a piece of writing from courant to soutenu.

For adding a further point rather than contrasting one, use:

> *En outre, ce choix réduit les coûts.* — "Furthermore, this choice reduces costs."
>
> *Par ailleurs, il faut tenir compte des délais.* — "Moreover, timelines need to be taken into account."

## Anglicism trap 1: "c'est à propos de" vs. "il s'agit de"

English speakers naturally reach for a literal translation of "it's about..." — but "c'est à propos de" reads as a calque, noticeably influenced by English, in formal writing. The native formal construction is the impersonal "il s'agit de":

> ❌ *Ce rapport, c'est à propos de la sécurité.*
>
> ✅ *Il s'agit, dans ce rapport, de la sécurité.* / *Ce rapport traite de la sécurité.*

## Anglicism trap 2: "réaliser" vs. "se rendre compte de"

"Réaliser que..." meaning "to realise that..." is spreading through French under English influence, but it's still widely considered incorrect in careful writing. The traditional, unambiguously correct formal verb is "se rendre compte de/que":

> ❌ *J'ai réalisé que j'avais oublié mon passeport.*
>
> ✅ *Je me suis rendu compte que j'avais oublié mon passeport.*

## Anglicism trap 3: "actuellement" — a false friend, not an anglicism, but just as dangerous

"Actuellement" looks like "actually" but means "currently." Using it to mean "actually" produces a sentence that means something entirely different from what you intended:

> *Actuellement, je travaille à Lyon.* — "I currently work in Lyon." (NOT "I actually work in Lyon.")

"Actually," in the sense of correcting or clarifying, is "en fait":

> *En fait, je ne travaille plus à Lyon.* — "Actually, I no longer work in Lyon."

## The underlying habit

All three traps share a cause: translating English sentence structure word-for-word instead of reaching for the phrase French formal writing actually uses. The fix isn't memorising a blacklist — it's reading enough real formal French (news, official letters, well-edited essays) that the native phrasing starts to feel more natural than the calque.
