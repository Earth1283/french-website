# Familier, Courant, Soutenu: The Same Word, Three Times

The fastest way to learn register is through triplets: one everyday concept, three real words. French does this constantly, and once you've internalised a dozen triplets, the *pattern* starts to transfer to words you've never seen.

## Eating, working, leaving

> *Tu bouffes avec nous ?* (familier) / *Tu manges avec nous ?* (courant) / *Souhaitez-vous vous restaurer ?* (soutenu) — "Are you eating with us?"

"Bouffer" isn't rude exactly, but it's unmistakably casual — the verbal equivalent of "grub" or "scarf down." "Se restaurer" is what a motorway sign or a wedding invitation says; using it with friends reads as a joke.

> *Je bosse demain.* (familier) / *Je travaille demain.* (courant) / *Elle œuvre pour la paix.* (soutenu) — "I work tomorrow." / "She works for peace."

Notice "œuvrer" doesn't casually mean "to have a job" — it means labouring toward something noble. Calling your shift at the supermarket "œuvrer" would sound like self-parody, which is itself a useful register joke once you know the rule you're breaking.

> *Je me casse.* (familier) / *Je pars.* (courant) / *Je m'en vais.* (soutenu-ish, literary) — "I'm leaving."

"Se casser" to someone you don't know well can land as rude. "Partir" is always the safe, neutral choice. "S'en aller" leans literary — you'll meet it in songs and novels more than group chats.

## Things and people

> *sa bagnole* / *sa voiture* / *son véhicule* — "his car"

A police report says *véhicule*. Your friend describing their new hatchback says *bagnole* or, neutrally, *voiture*. Nobody says *véhicule* about their own car unless they're being funny or filling out insurance paperwork.

> *ce mec* / *cet homme* / *ce monsieur* — "this guy / man / gentleman"

Register here tracks respect as much as formality: *ce monsieur* is what you'd say about someone's elderly father, *ce mec* about a stranger your own age.

## Judgement words

> *C'est chiant.* / *C'est ennuyeux.* / *C'est fastidieux.* — "It's annoying / boring / tedious."

"Chiant" comes from "chier" — it's genuinely vulgar, completely normal between friends, and a real misstep in front of your boss. "Fastidieux" is what you'd write in a formal complaint about a bureaucratic process.

## The pattern to internalise

Across all these pairs, a few signals repeat:

- Soutenu words are often longer, sometimes with Latin-heavy roots (*se restaurer*, *vociférer*, *fastidieux*).
- Familier words are often shorter and can look "clipped" (*bosser*, *bouffer*, *fric*).
- Courant sits in the middle and is almost always the safest default when you're unsure.

You don't need to memorise hundreds of triplets. You need enough of them, and enough exposure, that your ear starts predicting register the way it already predicts gender.
