# The Three Registers of French

Every language has registers — the same idea dressed for different rooms. English speakers slide between "gonna grab a bite," "I'm going to eat," and "I shall partake of a meal" without thinking about it. French speakers do the same thing constantly, but the gap between the levels is wider, more codified, and more consequential than in English. Get it wrong and you don't just sound casual — you sound like you don't know what you're doing.

French teaching materials usually name three registers:

- **Familier** — friends, family, bars, group chats. Contains slang, dropped grammar, and words you will never see in print.
- **Courant** — the default. Newspapers, colleagues, strangers, most conversation. What this course teaches unless it says otherwise.
- **Soutenu** — formal writing, official speech, literature, job applications, a wedding toast to your new in-laws.

## Why this matters more in French than in English

English registers shift mostly through word choice and tone. French registers shift through word choice *and* grammar. Dropping "ne" in negation, swapping "nous" for "on," skipping the subjunctive where it's required — these aren't stylistic flourishes, they're structural markers that a French ear clocks instantly. A B2 learner who can conjugate perfectly but only ever speaks in flawless soutenu sentences will sound like a legal document that learned to talk. One who only knows familier will struggle to write a cover letter, request a refund by email, or make a good impression on someone's parents.

> *Ça va ?* — familier/courant, "You good?"
>
> *Comment allez-vous ?* — soutenu, "How are you keeping?"

Both mean roughly the same thing. Using the second one at a bar with friends your own age is not "more polite" — it's *odd*, the linguistic equivalent of showing up to a barbecue in a tuxedo.

## The core skill at B2

Up to this point you've mostly learned *courant* — the safe, all-purpose middle register. At B2, the job shifts: you need to **recognise** familier and soutenu when you meet them, even if you never produce much soutenu yourself. That means:

- Spotting when a word or sentence signals "this is casual" or "this is formal."
- Knowing a handful of real familier/soutenu equivalents for common courant words.
- Not panicking when a French colleague's email opens with "Nous vous prions de bien vouloir..." or a friend texts "chais pas, on verra."

This lesson builds the vocabulary triplets — the same concept in three outfits. The next two lessons go deeper: how French is actually *spoken* day to day, and how to read and write proper formal French. Think of register not as a rulebook but as a wardrobe: you need to know what's in it before you can dress for the room you're walking into.
