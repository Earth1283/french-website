# Spotting Register in Context

Knowing individual triplets is step one. Step two is reading a whole sentence and deciding, quickly, what register it belongs to — because in real life nobody hands you a word in isolation. They hand you a text message, an email, or a sentence someone just said to your face, and you need to know how to answer *in kind*.

## Clues beyond vocabulary

Register isn't only about which synonym someone picked. Several other signals stack up together:

- **Dropped grammar** — no "ne" in negation, "on" instead of "nous" — signals familier, almost regardless of vocabulary.
- **Sentence length and structure** — soutenu favours longer, subordinated sentences; familier favours short, fragmented ones.
- **Address terms** — *tu* vs *vous*, first names vs *Monsieur/Madame*, nicknames.
- **Set phrases** — certain fixed expressions are register flags on their own: "il s'agit de" (soutenu), "du coup" (familier), "veuillez agréer" (very soutenu, letter-closing only).

> *T'as pas vu mes clés, par hasard ?* — familier: dropped "ne," dropped "tu es" → "t'as," casual "par hasard" tacked on.
>
> *Auriez-vous aperçu mes clés ?* — soutenu: conditional + inversion, same literal question.

Same request, two completely different social postures — one for a roommate, one for a stranger whose desk you're standing next to.

## Mixed registers are normal — and meaningful

Real speech and writing often blend registers on purpose. A journalist might drop one familier word into an otherwise courant article for effect; a friend might suddenly go formal to make a joke, or to signal they're being serious. Recognising *when* someone shifts register tells you something the words alone don't:

> *Bon, je vais me permettre de vous donner mon avis.* — Formal phrasing ("je vais me permettre de") used jokingly among friends usually signals mock self-importance, not real formality.

## A practical filter

When you meet an unfamiliar sentence, ask three quick questions:

1. **Would I write this in an email to my landlord?** If yes, it's courant or soutenu.
2. **Does it drop grammar or use slang I've flagged as familier?** If yes, it's familier — match it if you want to sound natural, avoid it in writing.
3. **Does it use inversion, "il s'agit de," subjunctive after formal triggers, or unusually long connectors?** That's soutenu — expected in a cover letter, jarring in a text to a friend.

This isn't about judging any register as "better." Soutenu in the wrong place sounds pompous; familier in the wrong place sounds careless. The goal at B2 is *control*: choosing your register on purpose, and understanding the choice someone else just made when they spoke to you.
