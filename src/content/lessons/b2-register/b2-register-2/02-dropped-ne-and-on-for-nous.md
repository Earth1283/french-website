# Dropped ne, and On for Nous

Two changes account for more of the gap between "textbook French" and "spoken French" than everything else combined. Master these two and a huge amount of real conversation stops sounding garbled.

## The vanishing "ne"

Standard negation is "ne... pas" wrapped around the verb: *je ne sais pas*. In casual speech, the "ne" is very often dropped entirely, leaving "pas" (or *jamais*, *rien*, *plus*...) to carry the whole negative meaning on its own.

> *Je ne sais pas.* → *Je sais pas.* — "I don't know."
>
> *Il n'y a plus de pain.* → *Y a plus de pain.* — "There's no more bread."
>
> *Ce n'est pas grave.* → *C'est pas grave.* — "It's not a big deal."

Nothing about this is sloppy or "wrong" grammar in the way a native speaker experiences it — it's simply how the sentence is spoken. The written standard keeps "ne"; formal speech keeps "ne"; a text to a friend usually won't have it. Your job: **write with "ne," but don't be thrown when you don't hear it.**

## Chais pas: the double contraction

Push the dropped "ne" one step further and you get one of the most common spoken forms of all: *chais pas*, from *je sais pas*, itself from *je ne sais pas*. The "je" and "sais" fuse into a single syllable. It sounds nothing like its spelling suggests, which is exactly why it trips up learners who've only seen it written.

> *Chais pas, on verra.* — "Dunno, we'll see."

## On instead of nous

The second big shift: in spoken French, *on* almost completely replaces *nous* as the word for "we." *Nous* survives in writing, in formal speech, and as the object/stressed pronoun (*nous, on y va ?* — using both together is itself a common spoken pattern for emphasis). But as the subject of a verb meaning "we," *on* is overwhelmingly what people actually say.

> *Nous allons au cinéma.* (written/formal) vs. *On va au cinéma.* (spoken) — "We're going to the cinema."

## The trap: on takes il/elle conjugation

Here's the part that catches learners even after they've accepted that *on* means "we": grammatically, *on* is a third-person singular pronoun, like *il* or *elle*. The verb conjugates accordingly — **not** in the *nous* form.

> ✅ *On va au marché.* — correct: *va*, same form as *il va*.
>
> ❌ *On allons au marché.* — incorrect: this borrows the *nous* ending onto *on*, a mistake even some native children make while learning to write, and one textbooks flag heavily.

This single rule — *on* + third-person-singular verb — is worth drilling until it's automatic, because it's both extremely common and consistently mis-produced by learners who mentally treat *on* as a stand-in for the *nous* conjugation rather than for the *nous* meaning.
