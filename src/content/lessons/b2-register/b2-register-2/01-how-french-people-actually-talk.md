# How French People Actually Talk

Textbook French and spoken French are two different animals wearing the same coat. Every negation gets its full "ne... pas," every "nous" stays "nous," every sentence resolves neatly. Walk into a real French conversation and you'll hear something faster, more contracted, and full of little words that no textbook bothered to teach you because they're technically "incorrect."

They're not incorrect. They're familier register — the spoken default among friends, family, and increasingly, in informal texts and social media. Understanding it is a listening-comprehension survival skill: if you only know the "correct" written forms, real conversation will feel like it's moving at double speed.

## Reading vs. producing

This lesson has a specific, narrow goal: **recognition first, production second.** You should be able to instantly understand *"chais pas, on verra"* even if you keep writing *"je ne sais pas, on verra"* yourself for now. Producing spoken-French forms confidently takes more immersion than one lesson can give you — but understanding them the moment you hear them is achievable right now, and it's the more urgent skill.

> *Written / careful speech:* Je ne sais pas si nous avons le temps.
>
> *Fast spoken French:* Chais pas si on a le temps.

Same sentence, same meaning, radically different surface. A learner who's only met the first version will genuinely struggle with the second, even at a solid grammar level — this is one of the most common reasons intermediate learners feel like they "understand French but not French people."

## What actually changes

Spoken French doesn't add new grammar so much as it strips and contracts existing grammar:

- The negative "ne" disappears almost entirely in casual speech.
- "Nous" is replaced by "on" for "we" — constantly, in nearly every context short of a formal speech.
- Pronoun + verb combinations fuse: "tu es" → "t'es," "tu as" → "t'as," "il y a" → "y a."
- A handful of filler words (*du coup*, *genre*, *en mode*, sentence-final *quoi*) pepper ordinary sentences the way English uses "like," "so," and "you know."

You may already know some slang words from verlan (like *ouf* or *meuf*) — that's vocabulary. This lesson is different: it's about the *grammar and rhythm* of casual speech, which shows up even in sentences with zero slang in them.

## Why this is worth a whole lesson

At B1 you learned to build correct sentences. At B2, the job is to stop being confused when a French person doesn't. The next two chapters cover the specific mechanics — dropped "ne" and "on," then the fillers — and the final chapter gives you real dialogue to decode.
