# Fillers and Verbal Tics

Beyond grammar contractions, spoken French is stitched together with small filler words that carry little or no literal meaning but do real conversational work — hesitating, softening a statement, adding emphasis, or just filling space while the speaker's brain catches up with their mouth. English does the same thing with "like," "so," "you know," and "I mean." French has its own set, and recognising them stops you from over-analysing words that were never meant to be analysed.

## Du coup

Originally "du coup" meant "as a consequence of that" — a genuine logical connector. In contemporary spoken French it has drifted into an all-purpose "so..." that can open almost any sentence, whether or not real causation is involved.

> *Du coup, tu viens ou pas ?* — "So, are you coming or not?" (no real "consequence" implied — it's just a conversational pivot)

Used sparingly it's fine even in fairly neutral speech; used in every second sentence, it marks very casual register and can sound like a verbal tic, the French equivalent of someone saying "so" or "like" constantly in English.

## Genre

"Genre" literally means "type" or "genre" (as in film genre), but as a filler it works exactly like English "like" — inserted mid-sentence for hesitation or approximation.

> *Il est arrivé, genre, une heure en retard.* — "He showed up, like, an hour late."

## En mode

A newer, trendier construction: "en mode X" describes a state or vibe, often for comic or exaggerated effect.

> *Elle est en mode stressée.* — "She's totally stressed out."
>
> *On est parti en mode aventure.* — "We went full adventure mode."

Treat this one as vocabulary to *recognise* rather than a structure you need to produce fluently — it's popular among younger speakers and shifts quickly with trends.

## Quoi (sentence-final)

You already know "quoi" as the question word "what." At the end of a statement, it does something completely different: it's a verbal shrug, roughly "y'know" or "I mean," adding no new information.

> *C'est nul, quoi.* — "It's rubbish, y'know."

## Ouais

The casual version of "oui." Completely normal in speech, absent from formal writing.

> *— Tu viens ? — Ouais, carrément.* — "Are you coming? — Yeah, definitely."

## Reading, not producing

As with the grammar changes in the previous chapter, the goal here is recognition. Sprinkling *genre* and *du coup* into every sentence you produce can sound try-hard if it's not backed by natural rhythm — but failing to recognise them when a French friend uses them will make you miss half of what they said.
