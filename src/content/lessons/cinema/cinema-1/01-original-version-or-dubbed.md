# Original Version or Dubbed: Choosing Your Film

Buying cinema tickets in France takes less than a minute once you know three things: which version you want, which showing you want, and how many seats you need. The vocabulary is short, and it prevents the most common mistake, which is spending two hours watching a film in a language you did not choose.

## *VO* and *VF*: the decision that matters

Most foreign films are shown in one of two forms. ***La version originale (VO)*** keeps the original soundtrack, usually English for an American film, and adds French subtitles. ***La version française (VF)*** replaces the dialogue with French voices. Listings often use ***VOST***, short for *version originale sous-titrée*, which also means original version with subtitles.

> *Je voudrais voir le film en version originale, s'il vous plaît.* — I would like to see the film in the original version, please.

French dubbing is professionally made, and many people prefer it for children's films. For an English-language film you want to hear the actors' own voices, though, choose VO or VOST.

## Asking for a showing

*Une séance* is a screening: the scheduled time when a film starts. Ask:

> *À quelle heure est la prochaine séance ?* — What time is the next showing?

*Séance* also means a session, such as a therapy session. In a cinema queue, the meaning is always the film.

## Buying your seats

In a cinema, a ticket is usually called ***une place***, which literally means a place or a seat. You will also hear *un billet*, so you will not be misunderstood if you use it. To ask for several tickets, put the number before *places*:

> *Deux places, s'il vous plaît.* — Two tickets, please.

Finally, *la salle* is the auditorium. Your ticket will say something like *salle 3*, which means screen three. Seats are often allocated, so check the row and seat number when you collect your ticket.

Before you reach the counter, decide on three things: the version, the time, and the number of seats. Then the request is a single sentence.
