# The Traps at the Ticket Desk

Cinema French is mostly harmless. The mistakes that do damage are small: a word that sounds familiar but means something else, or an assumption about how a listing works. Here are the ones English speakers fall into most often.

## Trap 1: assuming “session” means “session”

English speakers may hear *séance* and think of a session of something. In a cinema, *une séance* is a screening at a particular time. The word is not dangerous, but using *session* as an English translation will confuse the person at the counter.

## Trap 2: asking for the wrong language

A common request is *Est-ce que le film est en anglais ?* (“Is the film in English?”). The answer may be yes, and you may still end up with a dubbed film if the listing says *VF*. Ask for the version directly with ***VO*** or ***VOST***. Those words are what the staff and the listing use.

## Trap 3: taking the listing at face value

Small cinemas may show only the dubbed version, while large city cinemas usually show both. If the listing shows *VF* only, you can ask: *Y a-t-il une version originale ?* (“Is there an original version?”) The *y* means “there,” and it is a natural question even if the answer is no.

## Trap 4: translating *ticket* word for word

*Un ticket* exists in French, but it usually refers to a receipt, a parking ticket, or a metro ticket. For a film, *une place* is the natural choice. If you hear *un billet*, that is also correct, so do not panic when the seller uses it.

## Trap 5: the film is sold out

If the answer is *Il n'y a plus de places*, the cinema has no seats left. Note the phrase: it means “there are no more seats,” not that the cinema is closed. Ask for the next showing instead: *Il y a une autre séance ?*

The rule underneath all five traps is simple: when a word feels familiar, check what it means in the place where you are standing.
