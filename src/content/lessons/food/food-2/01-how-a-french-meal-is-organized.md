# How to Read the Meal Before the Menu

A French restaurant becomes easier when you understand its structure. You are not only decoding food words; you are choosing between ways of ordering and recognizing which stage of the meal the server is discussing.

## *La carte* and *le menu* are not the same

*La carte* is the full list of dishes—the object English speakers usually call “the menu.” *Un menu* often means a fixed-price combination, such as a starter and main course or a main course and dessert. You may see:

- *entrée + plat* — starter plus main course
- *plat + dessert* — main course plus dessert
- *entrée + plat + dessert* — all three courses

In French, ***entrée*** means the starter, not the main dish. The main course is ***le plat*** or ***le plat principal***. This one false friend can completely change what arrives at your table.

## The common course vocabulary

| French | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *l'apéritif / l'apéro* | pre-dinner drink, often with a small snack |
| *l'entrée* | starter |
| *le plat* | main course |
| *l'accompagnement* | side dish |
| *le fromage* | cheese course |
| *le dessert* | dessert |
| *le café* | usually espresso unless specified otherwise |

Not every meal contains every stage. At lunch, a two-course formula is common; at a casual café, you can order a single dish. Understanding the sequence simply helps you recognize questions such as *Vous avez choisi votre entrée ?* (“Have you chosen your starter?”).

## Useful labels on the page

*Fait maison* means “made in-house.” *Plat du jour* is the day’s special. *Prix net* means service and taxes are included in the displayed price. *Selon arrivage* means availability depends on what was delivered, especially for fish or market produce.

Before ordering, scan for the formula, identify the course categories, and decide whether you want the fixed option or dishes *à la carte*. Once that decision is made, the words you need become much simpler.
