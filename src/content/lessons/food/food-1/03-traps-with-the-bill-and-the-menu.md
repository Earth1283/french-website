# Traps with the Bill and the Menu

Coffee and food come with a few traps that surprise English speakers, especially around money and the bill. Most of them are not dangerous, but they can leave you waiting, confused, or slightly overcharged.

## Waiting for the bill

French waiters generally do not bring the bill unless you ask. This is not indifference. Lingering over a coffee is a normal part of the day, and the bill is treated as something you request when you are ready to go. To ask, say:

> *L'addition, s'il vous plaît.* — The bill, please.

Making eye contact and raising a hand is usually enough. Calling out loudly is not necessary.

## *La carte* versus *le menu*

These two words are the most common source of confusion at a restaurant. *La carte* is the full list, and *le menu* is a fixed-price set of courses. If you ask for *le menu*, you may get the set option even when you wanted to choose freely. Use *la carte* when you want to order à la carte.

## Service and tipping

Service is included in French restaurant prices by law, and you will often see *service compris* on the bill. Tipping is not expected, but rounding up or leaving a few coins is a kind gesture for good service. Do not leave a large tip as if you were in the United States.

## Checking the total

If something looks wrong, say so politely:

> *Pardon, il y a une erreur dans l'addition.* — Excuse me, there is a mistake in the bill.

Keep the tone calm. Waiters see errors every day, and an accusatory tone rarely helps.

## Avoiding the "grande" trap

English speakers sometimes ask for a "large coffee" using American vocabulary. In France, there is no standard large coffee. If you want more volume, ask for *un café allongé* or a *grand crème*, which is a larger milky coffee. Do not expect a takeaway cup the size of a bucket.

Most of these traps come from assuming that French restaurants work like home. They do not, but they are consistent once you know the pattern.
