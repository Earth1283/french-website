# Ordering Coffee at a Counter or Terrace

Coffee in France is a small, daily ritual, and the way you order it says more about you than you might expect. The first rule is simple: greet the person before you order. Skipping *Bonjour* is considered rude, even if the rest of your French is perfect.

## The basic order

The most useful sentence in this chapter is:

> *Un café, s'il vous plaît.* — A coffee, please.

In France, *un café* means an espresso. It is small, strong, and served in a tiny cup. If you want something larger, *un café allongé* is an espresso lengthened with hot water, closer to what many English speakers mean by a regular coffee.

For breakfast, locals often order *un café au lait*, which is coffee with hot milk, usually served in a bowl or large cup. It is what people dunk their croissants into. You will rarely see it after the morning.

## Standing or sitting

Prices change depending on where you drink. Standing at the bar, *au comptoir*, is the cheapest option. Sitting on the terrace, *en terrasse*, costs more because you are paying for the seat and the view. Neither choice is wrong. Just know that the price on the board may reflect the location.

## Pastries and the croissant

> *Un croissant, s'il vous plaît.* — A croissant, please.

Pronounce it *kwa-SAHN*, with a nasal ending and no hard *t*. The pronunciation signals that you have paid attention, and it is one of the cheapest ways to sound more fluent.

## Asking for the menu

If you are sitting down, you may want the food list too:

> *La carte, s'il vous plaît.* — The menu, please.

*La carte* is the full list of dishes. Since *le menu* means a fixed-price meal, asking for *le menu* may get you something different from what you wanted.

Greeting, ordering, and a polite *s'il vous plaît* are enough to start. Everything else builds on that frame.
