# The Grammar Behind Going Places

The phrases in the last chapter look like memorized chunks, and you can use them that way. But a few small rules explain why they work, and those rules let you swap in other places and verbs without starting over.

## *Aller à* and the small contractions

*Aller* means "to go," and *à* means "to" or "at." The article after *à* depends on the noun that follows, and sometimes it contracts:

- *à + le → au* — *au musée* (to the museum)
- *à + les → aux* — *aux toilettes* (to the toilets)
- *à + la → à la* — *à la gare* (to the station)
- *à + l' → à l'* — *à l'aéroport* (to the airport)

Cities usually take no article at all: *à Paris*, *à Lyon*. Saying *à le musée* is the most common error English speakers make here, and it is easy to avoid once you recognize the *au* pattern.

## The imperative with *vous*

*Appelez* and *Compostez* are commands, but they follow a simple rule. For *-er* verbs, the imperative *vous* form is the same as the present *vous* form. *Vous appelez* means "you call," and *Appelez !* means "call!" Politeness comes from the *vous* form, which is why you hear it in service situations.

When you add *moi* to the end of a command, join it with a hyphen: *appelez-moi* ("call me"). The hyphen belongs there, and it also tells you that the pronoun is a direct object.

## The question word order

*À quelle heure part le train ?* uses a noun subject placed after the verb. When the subject is a noun, French often keeps this order in casual speech. The more formal version is *À quelle heure le train part-il ?*, but you will hear the shorter form everywhere.

## Gender and the adjective

*Un aller simple* uses the masculine noun *aller* ("outward journey") with an adjective after it. French adjectives normally follow nouns. *Un aller* is masculine, so *simple* stays unchanged. Compare *une gare* (feminine) and *un train* (masculine) when you build phrases of your own.

## Pronunciation notes

*Taxi* stresses the final syllable: *tak-SI*. The *x* is pronounced, and the final vowel is a clear *ee*. *Aller* ends with a silent *r*, so it sounds like *ah-lay*. In *l'hôtel*, the *h* is silent and the *l'* elides with the vowel that follows.

Once you know these rules, *Je voudrais aller à la gare* becomes a template. Replace the place, keep the frame, and the sentence works.
