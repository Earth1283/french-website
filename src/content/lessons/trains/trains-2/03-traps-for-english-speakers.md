# Five Traps English Speakers Fall Into on Trains

The mistakes that cost time at a French station are predictable. Each one comes from a word that looks harmless in English and behaves differently in French.

## *Voiture* is not always a car

A traveller who arrives at the platform looking for a *voiture* will find no automobiles, only railway carriages. In a train context *la voiture* means the carriage, and only the number on the ticket will help you find it. Read the context before you assume the word means "car."

## *Quai* is not only a riverside

*Le quai* can also mean the embankment along a river, and Parisians walk along the *quais de Seine* on purpose. At a station, it means the platform. Do not look for the river.

## *Je suis en retard* versus *Il est tard*

English speakers often say "I am late" for the train and mix up the time-of-day expression. *Il est tard* means "it is late" in the sense of "it is late at night." For the train, you need *Je suis en retard* or *Mon train est en retard*. Using the wrong one will make your sentence sound odd, though the staff will probably understand.

## *Rater* is the real verb for missing something

"I missed my connection" is *J'ai raté ma correspondance*. Do not translate "miss" with *manquer* in this context. *Manquer* means to lack or to miss a person you care about, as in *il me manque*, "I miss him." For a train, *rater* is precise.

## *Contrôle* is a check, not an authority

*Le contrôle* is the act of checking. *Le contrôleur* is the person who checks. The English word "controller" suggests someone who runs things, which is not the job. The inspector only wants to see that your ticket is valid, so keep it ready.

## The general rule

When an English word feels obvious, check whether the French word has the same gender, the same range of meanings, and the same grammar. Usually it does not, and the surprise is the reason you remember it.
