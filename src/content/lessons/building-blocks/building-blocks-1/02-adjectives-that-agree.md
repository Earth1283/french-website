# Colors That Change Their Clothes

French adjectives agree with the noun they describe. A color is no exception. The masculine form is the base, and the feminine usually adds an *-e*. Plural nouns add an *-s*. This sounds like a rule for spelling, but it also changes how words sound, which is useful when you are listening.

## The basic pattern

> *Un pull vert* — a green sweater (masculine)
>
> *Une chemise verte* — a green shirt (feminine)
>
> *Des chaussures vertes* — green shoes (feminine plural)

*Vert* becomes *verte*, and the final *t* becomes audible. In speech, *vɛʁ* and *vɛʁt* are different sounds, which tells you the noun's gender even when you do not hear the article clearly.

## Colors that already end in *e*

*Noir* becomes *noire*, but the sound does not change. The same goes for *bleu*: *bleu* and *bleue* are pronounced alike. The spelling shows the agreement, but you cannot rely on your ear to detect it. Watch the article and the noun instead.

## Colors that never change

*Orange* and *marron* are invariable when used as adjectives: they keep one form for all genders and numbers. You say *une robe orange* and *des chaussures marron*. *Rose* (pink) already ends in *-e*, so it never changes for gender, but it does take an *-s* in the plural: *des chemises roses*. Yes, French made one exception to the exception.

## Feminine endings with a sound change

*Blanc* becomes *blanche*, and the feminine adds a clear *sh* sound: *blɑ̃ʃ*. *Gris* becomes *grise*, with the *z* sound audible. *Violet* becomes *violette*, with the *t* pronounced. Listen for these endings in shops, because they confirm the noun's gender. If you hear *une maison blanche*, you know the noun is feminine even before you catch the word *maison*.
