# Why Colors Are a Survival Skill

When you cannot remember the word for a thing, you can often describe it. "The bag" becomes "the red bag," and that is frequently enough for a shopkeeper, a waiter, or a stranger at the train station to point in the right direction. Colors give you a reliable way to identify what you mean.

## The basic phrases

> *C'est rouge.* — It's red.
>
> *Le sac bleu, s'il vous plaît.* — The blue bag, please.
>
> *Je cherche un pull noir.* — I'm looking for a black sweater.

Notice that the color comes after the noun. *Le sac bleu* does not mean "the blue bag" in the English order; it literally says "the bag blue." French adjectives generally follow what they describe, and colors are among the most consistent examples. You will learn the reasons in the next chapter.

## Colors you will use most

Start with the common ones: *rouge* (red), *bleu* (blue), *vert* (green), *noir* (black), *blanc* (white), *gris* (grey), and *jaune* (yellow). These cover most shopping conversations. *Marron* (brown) and *orange* are equally useful, especially for clothing and food.

## Pointing and describing together

Pointing still works, and it is more polite than hoping the other person understands your gesture. Combine the two: *Celui-là, le bleu.* (That one, the blue one.) *Celui-là* means "that one," and it lets you name a specific item without needing its name.

If you are unsure of the word for a color, describe it through something familiar: *comme le ciel* (like the sky) or *comme une banane* (like a banana). The French will usually recognize the color immediately, even if your phrasing is clumsy. The goal is to be understood, not to speak like a textbook.
