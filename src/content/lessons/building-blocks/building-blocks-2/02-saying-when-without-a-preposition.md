# Saying When Without an Extra Word

English often needs "on" or "every" to say when something happens. French frequently uses the day name with or without an article, and the difference changes the meaning. Getting this right is what separates "I'm coming Monday" from "I come on Mondays."

## One-off dates

When you mean a specific upcoming day, use the bare day name:

> *Je viens lundi.* — I'm coming on Monday.
>
> *On se voit vendredi ?* — Shall we meet on Friday?

Those sentences refer to a single occasion. The day name stands alone, without *le*, and the meaning is "this coming Monday" or "next Friday," depending on context.

## Habits and repeated events

To describe something that happens every week, add *le* before the day:

> *Le lundi, je fais du sport.* — On Mondays, I do sport.
>
> *Le samedi, le marché est ouvert.* — On Saturdays, the market is open.

The article *le* signals repetition. Compare *Je viens lundi* (one visit) with *Je viens le lundi* (every Monday). The difference is one word, but the schedule is entirely different.

## Stating the day

To say what day it is, use *Nous sommes* followed by the day:

> *Nous sommes mardi.* — It's Tuesday.

Do not say *Il est mardi*. That sentence is a common error, and it sounds like you are describing the time of day rather than the calendar. *Nous sommes* is the reliable form for the day of the week and the date.

## Before and after

*Avant-hier* means "the day before yesterday," and *après-demain* means "the day after tomorrow." Learn them as pairs with *hier* and *demain*. They help you talk about plans and past events without needing exact dates.
