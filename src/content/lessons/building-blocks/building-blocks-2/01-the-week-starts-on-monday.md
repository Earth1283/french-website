# The Week Starts on Monday

In France, the calendar begins on *lundi* and ends on *dimanche*. This is not a small detail. Many French diaries, timetables, and bus schedules follow that order, and so do the shops. Once you know the sequence, planning a visit or confirming an appointment becomes much easier.

## The seven days in order

> *lundi, mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi, samedi, dimanche*

Learn the sequence as a chant, not as seven separate words. When someone says *Nous sommes mercredi*, you should know that *jeudi* is tomorrow. When you need to cancel something on *vendredi*, you should already know that *samedi* and *dimanche* follow.

Days of the week are never capitalized in French, even at the beginning of a sentence. You will see *lundi* in lowercase in a headline, a menu, or a calendar, and the rule applies to every day.

## The three relative days

*Aujourd'hui* means "today," *demain* means "tomorrow," and *hier* means "yesterday." These three are the words most people use to make plans and talk about the recent past.

> *Aujourd'hui, c'est lundi.* — Today is Monday.
>
> *Demain, je travaille.* — Tomorrow, I'm working.
>
> *Hier, il a plu.* — Yesterday, it rained.

*Aujourd'hui* is spelled with an apostrophe and an *h*. Pronounce it as one word, *oh-zhoor-dwee*, and do not split it into *au jour d'hui* when you speak. The spelling reflects history, but speakers treat it as a single unit.

## Sunday in particular

*Dimanche* is the last day of the French week, not the first. Many shops, markets, and offices are closed or work reduced hours. If you need something on a Sunday, check ahead, or look for a *pharmacie de garde*, which stays open on rotation.
