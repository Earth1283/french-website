# Day Traps and Quick Fixes

The days of the week are some of the easiest vocabulary to learn and some of the easiest to get slightly wrong. The traps below appear in almost every beginner's messages, so it helps to recognize them before they become habit.

## Trap one: capitalizing the day

English capitalizes days of the week, and the habit follows you into French. Write *lundi* and *samedi* in lowercase, always. This applies even when the day starts a sentence: *Lundi, je travaille* is correct.

## Trap two: *il est* versus *nous sommes*

Saying *Il est lundi* sounds like a translation of "It is Monday," but French uses *Nous sommes lundi* for the day of the week. *Il est* is for the time, as in *Il est dix heures*. Keep the two patterns separate.

## Trap three: *demain* alone versus *demain matin*

*Demain* means "tomorrow." If you want "tomorrow morning," add *matin*: *demain matin*. English speakers often say "tomorrow" when they mean an exact part of the day, and French expects that detail to be explicit.

## Trap four: forgetting that Sunday is closed

Learners sometimes assume shops are open on the day they name. In France, *dimanche* often means closed doors, especially for smaller shops. If your plan depends on opening hours, ask before the weekend: *Vous êtes ouvert le dimanche ?* (Are you open on Sundays?)

## A quick check

Before sending a message with a day in it, ask yourself three questions. Is the day lowercase? Did I use *nous sommes* or *il est*? Did I mean one occasion or a habit? These three checks take seconds, and they catch most mistakes. When you are unsure, *à demain* is a safe way to end any conversation about tomorrow, and it sounds natural.
