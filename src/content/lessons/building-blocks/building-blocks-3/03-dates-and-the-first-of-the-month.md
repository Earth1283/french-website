# Dates: Day First, and the Special First

French writes dates in the order day, then month. "March 5" becomes *le cinq mars*. The phrase always starts with *le*, and the day is a cardinal number, not an ordinal, with one exception: the first of the month.

## Ordinary dates

> *le cinq mars* — the 5th of March
>
> *le vingt juillet* — the 20th of July
>
> *le deux février* — the 2nd of February

For the 2nd through the 31st, use the cardinal number: *deux*, *trois*, *vingt*. Do not use *deuxième* or *cinquième* for dates. Those forms are ordinal numbers, used for rankings and floors, not calendar dates.

## The first of the month

The first day of the month is the exception. Use *premier*:

> *le premier janvier* — the 1st of January
>
> *le premier mai* — the 1st of May

*Premier* is the only ordinal you need for dates. You will see it on New Year's Day, on posters for May Day, and on official forms. Shortening it to *1er* is common in writing, but in speech say the full word.

## Asking and answering

To ask the date, use one of these:

> *Quelle est la date aujourd'hui ?* — What is today's date?
>
> *On est le combien ?* — What's the date? (informal)

To answer, use *Nous sommes le* followed by the date: *Nous sommes le quatorze juin.* Notice that *Nous sommes le* works for dates, but *Nous sommes* alone works for the day of the week, as you saw in the days-of-the-week chapter.

## Writing and saying the same date

When you fill in a form, the day comes first, then the month, then the year. A date like *15/10/2026* is read as *le quinze octobre deux mille vingt-six*. Learn that order, and forms in French will become predictable rather than a source of mistakes.
