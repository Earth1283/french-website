# Fact, Opinion and Wishful Thinking: Practice

## One final listening habit

When you meet a subjunctive in a podcast, article, or conversation, do not merely translate the verb. Pause and find the trigger or the speaker's stance. Is somebody demanding an action, reacting emotionally, questioning truth, comparing possibilities, or searching for a not-yet-identified thing? Then say the clause again with an indicative version and ask what changed in meaning. This habit develops judgment rather than fear. At advanced level, the subjunctive is most useful when it lets you hear a speaker's distance, desire, evaluation, or uncertainty before they explain it directly.

This final practice is about meaning. Read the situations first, then notice why each mood appears. French speakers do not change mood for decoration; they signal their relationship to the information.

## The apartment search

> **Ana:** *Je cherche un appartement qui soit près du métro.*  
> **Luc:** *Je connais un appartement qui est près du métro, mais il coûte cher.*  
> **Ana:** *Je ne pense pas qu'il soit dans mon budget.*  
> **Luc:** *C'est le plus joli que j'aie visité cette année.*

Ana's apartment is wanted but not identified, hence *soit*. Luc's apartment is specific, hence *est*. Ana doubts affordability, hence *soit*. The superlative frames a subjective evaluation, hence *aie visité*.

## Spot the mistake

1. *Je crois qu'il soit prêt.* → normally *Je crois qu'il est prêt.*
2. *Je ne crois pas qu'il est prêt.* → *Je ne crois pas qu'il soit prêt.*
3. *Je cherche la personne qui puisse...* → use *peut* if the person is known and specific.
4. *C'est le meilleur que j'ai vu* → formal standard: *que j'aie vu*.

## Self-check

- Certainty: *Je sais qu'il ___ là.* (*est*.)
- Doubt: *Je doute qu'il ___ là.* (*soit*.)
- Desired person: *quelqu'un qui ___ aider* (*puisse*.)
- Known person: *quelqu'un qui ___ aider* (*peut*.)

If you can explain the speaker's stance, you can choose the mood. That explanation is your best defence against both guesswork and grammar folklore.

For extra practice, turn each indicative sentence in the dialogue into a doubtful version and explain the resulting mood change. Then reverse it. This two-way exercise proves that grammar follows the speaker's viewpoint rather than a memorised list of supposedly magical verbs.
