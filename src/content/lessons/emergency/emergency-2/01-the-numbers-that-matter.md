# The Numbers That Matter Most in an Emergency

In a crisis, nobody needs your opinion about the French number system. They need a number they can dial and a number they can say. Start with the short list of emergency numbers, and learn to say each one until it sounds routine.

## The core emergency numbers

| Number | French | Service |
|---|---|---|
| *15* | *quinze* | SAMU, medical emergency |
| *17* | *dix-sept* | Police |
| *18* | *dix-huit* | Fire brigade (*pompiers*) |
| *112* | *cent douze* | European emergency number |

The number *112* works across the European Union, and in France it connects to the same emergency services. Calling 112 is a good default if you are not sure which service you need, although 15, 17, and 18 go straight to the right people.

## Saying a number you need to give

When you tell an operator a number, say it at a steady pace. Do not rush the digits. A common approach is to say the number in the order it appears and then confirm it:

> *Mon numéro est le zéro six, douze, trente-quatre, cinquante-six, soixante-dix-huit.* — My number is oh six, twelve, thirty-four, fifty-six, seventy-eight.

French mobile numbers are read in pairs, so this format is the one people will expect. If the operator asks you to repeat, say *Pouvez-vous répéter le numéro ?* — no shame in it.

## Why this matters

Emergency operators have seconds, and they are trained to think in these numbers. If you say *quinze* clearly, you have already told them the category of your problem. The number is information before it is a phone call.

Practice these four numbers until you can say them without hesitation. Stress and fatigue make the simple words the first casualties.
