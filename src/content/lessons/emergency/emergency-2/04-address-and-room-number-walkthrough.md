# Giving an Address and a Room Number Under Pressure

This chapter puts the numbers to work in two realistic situations: calling for help for a friend, and telling the front desk of your hotel where you are. Read each exchange, then say the key lines aloud until they feel automatic.

## Calling for a friend who has fallen

> **You:** *Allô, j'ai besoin d'aide. Mon ami est tombé.*
>
> **Operator:** *Quel est votre numéro de téléphone ?*
>
> **You:** *Zéro six, douze, trente-quatre, cinquante-six, soixante-dix-huit.*
>
> **Operator:** *Où êtes-vous ?*
>
> **You:** *Au 14, rue des Écoles. Au troisième étage.*

*Au quatorze* uses the contraction *au*, which is the same pattern as *à + le*. Numbers in an address come before the street name, and *au troisième étage* gives the floor. Clear location details are the most useful thing you can provide.

## Telling the hotel your room number

> **You:** *Bonsoir. Je suis dans la chambre trois cent douze.*
>
> **Receptionist:** *Trois cent douze. D'accord.*
>
> **You:** *Pardon, c'est combien d'étages ?*
>
> **Receptionist:** *Quatre. Vous êtes au troisième.*

*Trois cent douze* has no hyphen after *cent* when another number follows. The number after the hundred is a separate word, so *trois cent douze* is 312. If the receptionist misses a detail, repeat it slowly and ask for confirmation.

## When you do not understand

Numbers get lost easily, even for native speakers. If an operator gives you a number you do not catch, say *Pardon, je n'ai pas compris le dernier chiffre.* Then ask them to repeat it in pairs or write it down.

## The pattern to memorize

1. Say the emergency or service you need.
2. Give your phone number, slowly and in pairs.
3. Give your location: street number, street name, and floor.
4. Confirm any number the other person gives you.

If the other person repeats a number differently, trust the repetition over your memory. Say *C'est bien ça ?* to check, and let them correct you if needed.
