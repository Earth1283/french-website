# Être Makes the Past Participle Agree

With *être*, the past participle behaves a little like an adjective. It agrees with the subject in gender and number. This is not decorative punctuation for grammar books; it is part of the spelling you write and often part of what you hear.

> *Paul est arrivé.* — Paul arrived.

> *Lina est arrivée.* — Lina arrived.

> *Paul et Mehdi sont arrivés.* — Paul and Mehdi arrived.

> *Lina et Zoé sont arrivées.* — Lina and Zoé arrived.

## The four endings

- masculine singular: *arrivé*
- feminine singular: *arrivée*
- masculine or mixed plural: *arrivés*
- feminine plural: *arrivées*

Add *-e* for feminine, *-s* for plural, and both for feminine plural. Pronunciation often hides the ending: *arrivé, arrivée, arrivés* sound alike. Do not use sound alone as your spelling supervisor.

## Reflexive verbs join this pattern

Reflexive verbs use *être*: *je me suis levé(e), nous nous sommes rencontrés*. Agreement is usually with the subject, although later grammar introduces a few object-placement exceptions. At this stage, write the clear subject agreement correctly before chasing edge cases.

> *Elle s'est levée tôt.* — She got up early.

> *Ils se sont rencontrés au travail.* — They met at work.

## The practical check

Before you finish an être sentence, ask:

1. Who did the action or movement?
2. Is that person feminine?
3. Is it plural?

Then add the required letters. The agreement belongs on the participle, not the auxiliary: never *elles sontes arrivées*. French has enough letters already; do not donate extra ones.

## Quick drill

- *Marie est partie.*
- *Mes sœurs sont parties.*
- *Julien et Ana sont restés.*
- *Nous sommes descendues.* (a group of women)

In a text message, write the endings even if nobody can hear them. Read the subject one more time before you send it: a single woman, a group of women, or a mixed group determines the visible form. That quick check prevents most agreement mistakes.

The rule may look fussy, but it makes written French precise: the participle tells the reader who moved, arrived, fell, or returned.
