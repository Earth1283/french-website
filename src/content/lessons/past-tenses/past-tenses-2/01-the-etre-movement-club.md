# The Être Movement Club Explained

## Learn patterns, then check meaning

Make two short lists in your notes: verbs that normally use *être* for the subject's movement or change, and verbs that use *avoir* for an action with an object. Then practise pairs such as *elle est montée* and *elle a monté les valises*. The contrast is more durable than a decorative “house” diagram because it tells you why the auxiliary changes. When a new verb appears, learn it with a complete past-tense sentence. That gives you the auxiliary, participle, pronunciation, and a context in one useful memory.

A small family of intransitive movement and change-of-state verbs forms the passé composé with *être*, not *avoir*. The list is worth learning because the pattern is common and because it introduces agreement. Think of people arriving, leaving, entering, being born or dying: the subject itself moves or changes state.

> *Je suis arrivé à huit heures.* — I arrived at eight.

> *Nous sommes partis tôt.* — We left early.

## Core verbs

- *aller, venir, arriver, partir, entrer, sortir*
- *monter, descendre, tomber, rester, retourner*
- *naître, mourir, devenir, revenir, rentrer*

The shortcut “all movement verbs use être” is tempting and wrong. *Marcher, courir, voyager* use *avoir*: *j'ai marché, elle a couru, nous avons voyagé*. Learn the actual group instead of trying to negotiate with geography.

> *Elle est née à Marseille.* — She was born in Marseille.

> *Ils sont revenus dimanche.* — They came back on Sunday.

## Some verbs can switch teams

*Sortir, monter, descendre, rentrer* can take *avoir* when they have a direct object. Compare the meaning, not the spelling.

> *Elle est sortie à midi.* — She went out at noon.

> *Elle a sorti son téléphone.* — She took out her phone.

> *Nous sommes montés.* — We went upstairs.

> *Nous avons monté les valises.* — We carried the suitcases upstairs.

## A safe building pattern

Subject + present form of *être* + past participle. Say the auxiliary clearly: *je suis, tu es, il est, nous sommes, vous êtes, ils sont*. Then check whether the participle must agree with the subject. That check is next, so do not panic early.

For now, recognise the contrast: *j'ai pris le train* but *je suis arrivé à Paris*. The train has an object; the traveller is the one who arrives.
