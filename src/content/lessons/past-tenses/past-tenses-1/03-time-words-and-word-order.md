# Past Actions Need Their Time Markers

The passé composé answers “what happened?” Time words answer “when?” Put the two together and your French stops sounding like a police report with the dates removed. A completed action normally keeps its time marker close to the verb or at the beginning of the sentence.

> *Hier, j'ai appelé ma sœur.* — Yesterday, I called my sister.

> *J'ai appelé ma sœur hier soir.* — I called my sister last night.

## Useful anchors

- *hier* — yesterday
- *avant-hier* — the day before yesterday
- *ce matin* — this morning
- *la semaine dernière* — last week
- *il y a deux jours* — two days ago
- *déjà* — already
- *encore* — again / still, depending on context

The compact pattern is reassuringly boring: subject, auxiliary, small adverb, past participle, then the rest. With *déjà*, *souvent*, *beaucoup* or *bien*, the adverb usually sits between the auxiliary and participle.

> *Nous avons déjà réservé les billets.* — We have already booked the tickets.

> *Elle a beaucoup travaillé.* — She worked a lot.

Longer time expressions such as *la semaine dernière* usually go at the end or front, not in the middle of the verb sandwich. Avoid translating English word by word: *I called yesterday* is not *j'ai hier appelé* in ordinary French.

## Negatives stay around the auxiliary

The passé composé still needs both pieces when it is negative. *Ne ... pas* wraps around the conjugated *avoir*, while the participle remains after it.

> *Je n'ai pas compris.* — I did not understand.

> *Ils n'ont jamais visité Lyon.* — They have never visited Lyon.

## Quick drill

Read these aloud, keeping the auxiliary and participle together:

- *J'ai fini hier.*
- *Tu as déjà mangé.*
- *Il n'a pas répondu.*
- *Nous avons beaucoup ri.*
- *Vous avez vu le film samedi.*
- *Elles ont encore oublié les clés.*

The goal is not to memorise a rigid slot machine. It is to make the two-part past tense feel like one unit, then place time information where a listener can catch it easily.
