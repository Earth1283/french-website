# Building the Passé Composé with Avoir

The passé composé is French's default way to talk about a finished past action: something that happened, once, and is over. It is called "compound" because it needs two pieces glued together — an auxiliary verb in the present tense, plus a past participle.

> *Sujet + avoir (présent) + participe passé*
>
> *J'ai mangé.* — I ate. / I have eaten.

Most French verbs — the overwhelming majority — build their passé composé with *avoir*. Only a short, closed list of verbs (mostly movement and reflexive verbs) uses *être* instead, and that list gets its own lesson next.

## The three regular endings

Regular verbs form their participle by chopping the infinitive ending and adding a fixed replacement, sorted by conjugation group:

- **-er verbs → -é**: *manger* → *mangé*, *parler* → *parlé*, *regarder* → *regardé*
- **-ir verbs → -i**: *finir* → *fini*, *choisir* → *choisi*, *réussir* → *réussi*
- **-re verbs → -u**: *vendre* → *vendu*, *attendre* → *attendu*, *perdre* → *perdu*

> *J'ai fini mon travail.* — I finished my work.
>
> *Tu as vendu ta voiture ?* — Did you sell your car?

Conjugate *avoir* normally for the subject — *j'ai, tu as, il/elle a, nous avons, vous avez, ils/elles ont* — and the participle itself never changes for person or number when the auxiliary is *avoir*. "Nous avons mangé" and "elles ont mangé" share the exact same participle, *mangé*. That rule flips with *être*, but that is next lesson's problem.

## Two English meanings, one French form

English speakers instinctively want to pick between "I ate" (simple past) and "I have eaten" (present perfect), as if French needed the same choice. It doesn't. *J'ai mangé* covers both; only the surrounding context tells you which nuance is meant.

> *J'ai mangé à midi.* — I ate at noon.
>
> *J'ai déjà mangé, merci.* — I've already eaten, thanks.

## Don't drop the auxiliary

In casual speech the participle alone can feel like it carries the whole meaning, but dropping *avoir* is not optional the way dropping *ne* sometimes is in negation. "Mangé" by itself is not a sentence — you always need the conjugated auxiliary in front of it, even when it seems redundant:

> *Il a fini.* — not *Il fini.*

Keep the two-part shape in your head: auxiliary first, participle second, always together. Once the avoir-plus-participle habit is automatic, the irregular participles in the next chapter are the only thing left to memorise.
