# Expensive Eyes and Long Arms

## Keep the whole chunk

Write each idiom on one side of a card and a realistic situation on the other. For *coûter les yeux de la tête*, imagine an overpriced flat or concert ticket; for *avoir le bras long*, imagine someone whose call gets a difficult appointment. Include the articles in the card: *les yeux de la tête*, *le bras long*. Fixed expressions are not build-your-own sentences. Hearing and repeating their shape is what makes them sound natural. If the situation is formal, switch to a neutral paraphrase; knowing when not to use an idiom is part of knowing it.

Body-part idioms are memorable because their images are absurd, but use them as whole chunks. Changing an article, preposition, or possessive often makes the phrase sound translated rather than French.

## Price and influence

> *Cet hôtel coûte les yeux de la tête.* — This hotel costs an arm and a leg.

The fixed phrase uses *les yeux de la tête*, not *mes yeux* or *des yeux*. It is informal but widespread, suitable for complaining to friends about a rent increase or an astonishing restaurant bill.

> *Elle a le bras long dans cette administration.* — She has influence in that administration.

*Avoir le bras long* means having useful connections or pull. It can sound admiring, suspicious, or mildly critical depending on tone. Do not use it as a literal description of a tall person's limbs; French will assume you are talking about influence.

## Social caution

Both phrases belong in conversation, not a formal report. In a professional email, say *ce projet est coûteux* rather than announcing that it devours heads. Idioms add colour, but register still decides whether the colour belongs on the wall.

## Quick drill

- *Ce sac coûte les yeux de la tête.*
- *Les billets nous ont coûté les yeux de la tête.*
- *Son oncle a le bras long.*
- *Elle connaît quelqu'un qui a le bras long.*

Learn the image and the situation together. The first is about a painfully high price; the second is about access and influence. The body itself is not the topic, which is the first useful rule of idioms.
