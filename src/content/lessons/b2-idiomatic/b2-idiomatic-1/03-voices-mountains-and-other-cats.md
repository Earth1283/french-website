# Voices, Mountains and Other Cats

Cats show up twice in this chapter, in two completely unrelated idioms, which is a useful reminder: French doesn't recycle animal imagery for related meanings the way you might expect. Each idiom has to be learned on its own terms.

## *Avoir un chat dans la gorge* — a frog in one's throat

Where English imagines a frog, French imagines a cat lodged in the throat, making your voice hoarse or catching mid-sentence. It's a mild, everyday phrase — the kind of thing you say before clearing your throat and continuing.

> *Excusez-moi, j'ai un chat dans la gorge, je reprends dans un instant.* — Excuse me, I have a frog in my throat, I'll continue in a moment.

There's no deeper meaning to hunt for here: it's simply the ordinary way to explain a raspy voice, used in meetings, phone calls, and casual conversation alike.

## *En faire tout un fromage* — making a mountain out of a molehill

Literally "to make a whole cheese out of it," this idiom describes someone blowing a minor issue out of proportion. It's interchangeable with *en faire toute une montagne*, but *fromage* is the more colloquial, distinctly French choice — appropriate, since French has over a thousand cheeses and evidently strong feelings about turning small things into large dairy products.

> *C'est juste une tache sur ta chemise, arrête d'en faire tout un fromage.* — It's just a stain on your shirt, stop making such a big deal out of it.

The phrase is almost always used to calm someone down or gently criticise an overreaction — it carries a slightly exasperated, affectionate tone rather than genuine anger.

## *Avoir d'autres chats à fouetter* — bigger fish to fry

Literally "to have other cats to whip" — an image nobody should take literally today. In practice, it simply means having more pressing priorities than whatever is currently being asked of you.

> *Je ne peux pas relire ton rapport maintenant, j'ai d'autres chats à fouetter.* — I can't proofread your report right now, I have other things to deal with.

It's a polite-ish way to decline without saying "I don't have time for you" — the responsibility shifts onto competing priorities rather than the other person's request.

## Putting them together

All three idioms are comfortably informal and safe in daily conversation, though *avoir un chat dans la gorge* is the most neutral — you could use it with your boss. The other two carry a slightly more familiar, conversational tone.

## Quick check

- Which idiom describes a hoarse voice? (*avoir un chat dans la gorge*)
- Which idiom means you have more urgent things to do? (*avoir d'autres chats à fouetter*)
