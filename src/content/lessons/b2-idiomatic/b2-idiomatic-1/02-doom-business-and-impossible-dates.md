# Doom, Business and Impossible Dates

This chapter covers three idioms that share a common thread: they all describe things that are finished, forbidden, or simply never going to happen. Vegetables and farmyard animals do the heavy lifting for all three.

## *Les carottes sont cuites* — it's all over

Literally "the carrots are cooked," this expression describes a situation with no way out: a plan that has failed, a deadline that has passed, a hope that is now pointless. Some trace it to soldiers' slang, where quick-to-prepare cooked carrots signalled that there was no more time left to act.

> *Quand j'ai vu les résultats de l'examen, j'ai compris que les carottes étaient cuites.* — When I saw the exam results, I understood it was all over.

The phrase is resigned rather than angry — it's what you say when you've accepted the outcome, not while you're still fighting it. The past tense is common too: *les carottes étaient cuites* describes a moment when the situation had already become hopeless.

## *Ce ne sont pas tes oignons* — none of your business

*Ce ne sont pas tes oignons* literally means "these are not your onions." It's a blunt way to tell someone to mind their own business, and register matters here: it's fine between siblings or close friends, but it would sound rude directed at a stranger, a colleague, or anyone you address as *vous*. In those situations, reach for the neutral *cela ne vous regarde pas* instead.

> *— Pourquoi tu as quitté ton travail ? — Ce ne sont pas tes oignons !* — "Why did you leave your job?" "That's none of your business!"

## *Quand les poules auront des dents* — when pigs fly

Hens do not grow teeth, which is exactly the point: *quand les poules auront des dents* means "never." It always sits in the future tense — *auront*, not *ont* — because the whole joke depends on describing a hypothetical future that will never arrive.

> *Il rangera sa chambre quand les poules auront des dents.* — He'll tidy his room when pigs fly.

English reaches for pigs; French reaches for toothy chickens. Both languages independently decided that farm animals doing anatomically impossible things is the funniest way to say "never."

## Register at a glance

- *Les carottes sont cuites* — informal, resigned, safe with friends and family.
- *Ce ne sont pas tes oignons* — informal and a little sharp; save it for people you know well.
- *Quand les poules auront des dents* — informal, humorous, never literal.

## Quick check

- Which idiom means "never"? (*quand les poules auront des dents*)
- Which tense does it always use, and why? (Future — *auront* — because the event will never actually happen.)
