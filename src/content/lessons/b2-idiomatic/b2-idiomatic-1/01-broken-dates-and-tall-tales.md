# Broken Dates and Tall Tales

French idioms rarely translate word for word, and the moment you try, you end up in a farmyard. This chapter opens the unit's menu of food and animal idioms with two of the most common: *poser un lapin* and *raconter des salades*. Neither has anything to do with rabbits or salad once you're actually using it — that's the whole trick of idiomatic French. You learn the fixed phrase as a single unit, the way a native speaker does, rather than translating it piece by piece.

## *Poser un lapin* — standing someone up

Literally, *poser un lapin à quelqu'un* means "to put down a rabbit for someone." Nobody agrees on the exact origin, but by the late 19th century the phrase already meant failing to show up for a date or appointment, leaving the other person waiting.

> *Elle m'a posé un lapin, je l'ai attendue devant le cinéma pendant vingt minutes.* — She stood me up; I waited for her outside the cinema for twenty minutes.

Two traps for English speakers. First, the verb is *poser*, not *mettre* — French idioms are picky about which verb they've fossilized with, and only *poser* works here. Second, the person who gets stood up is always marked with *à* or an indirect object pronoun: *il m'a posé un lapin*, never a bare *il a posé un lapin* floating without a victim.

## *Raconter des salades* — telling tall tales

*Raconter des salades* means to spin exaggerated, tangled stories — closer to "telling tall tales" or "making things up" than to a serious accusation of lying. The image is a salad: ingredients all mixed together, hard to untangle, a little messy.

> *Ne l'écoute pas, il raconte des salades depuis qu'il est petit.* — Don't listen to him, he's been telling tall tales since he was a kid.

Compare this with *mentir* (to lie), which is blunt and serious. *Raconter des salades* is lighter — it can describe a child's excuse, a friend's exaggerated fishing story, or a colleague's implausible reason for being late. Reach for *mentir* when you mean real deception; reach for *raconter des salades* when the story is more absurd than malicious.

## Why the verb matters as much as the noun

Both idioms are a reminder that French idioms are built as whole verb phrases, not swappable nouns. You cannot say *faire un lapin* or *dire des salades* and expect to be understood — the fixed combination of verb and noun is the idiom. Learn *poser un lapin* and *raconter des salades* as single locked units, exactly as you'd memorise a two-word verb in English like "show up" or "make up."
