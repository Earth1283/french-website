# Early Mornings, Forks and Bad Hair

## Register is the final test

Imagine two versions of the same message. To a friend: *Je ne suis pas du matin, ce film ne casse pas trois pattes à un canard.* To a client: *Je suis moins efficace tôt le matin; le film ne m'a pas particulièrement marqué.* Both are correct French, but only one belongs in each relationship. This is why idioms deserve a register label in your notes. They are not incorrect outside casual speech; they simply carry personality, humour, and social assumptions that a neutral paraphrase deliberately leaves behind.

The final group of idioms lets French speakers complain about mornings, impossible tasks, and unreliable appearances. As always, the image is fixed; do not translate an English phrase and hope French has made the same culinary choice.

> *Je ne suis pas du matin.* — I am not a morning person.

This is a neutral, very useful description of someone who functions poorly early in the day. It is safer than declaring that you have *le cafard* before coffee.

> *Ce n'est pas la mer à boire.* — It is not an impossible task.

Literally, “it is not the sea to drink.” Say it to encourage someone facing a manageable task: *Remplir ce formulaire, ce n'est pas la mer à boire.* Do not use it to dismiss a genuinely difficult problem; tone can turn encouragement into irritation.

> *Ça ne casse pas trois pattes à un canard.* — It is nothing special.

This is familiar and slightly comic. It evaluates something as mediocre or unremarkable. A formal review needs *ce n'est pas particulièrement remarquable* instead.

> *Être tiré à quatre épingles* means being very smartly dressed. The phrase is positive or teasing, depending on context.

## Quick drill

- *Je ne suis vraiment pas du matin.*
- *Allez, ce n'est pas la mer à boire.*
- *Son nouveau film ne casse pas trois pattes à un canard.*
- *Elle était tirée à quatre épingles pour le mariage.*

Each phrase gives you a natural way to add opinion and personality without slipping into slang you cannot control.
