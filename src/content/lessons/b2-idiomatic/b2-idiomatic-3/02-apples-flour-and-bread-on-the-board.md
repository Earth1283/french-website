# Apples, Flour and Bread on the Board

## Grammar around the idiom

Notice the ordinary grammar surrounding extraordinary pictures. You conjugate *tomber* with *être*: *elle est tombée dans les pommes*. You conjugate *avoir* in *on a du pain sur la planche*. With *se faire rouler dans la farine*, the person deceived is the reflexive subject and the deceiver can follow *par*. Make three versions in different tenses: yesterday, today, and tomorrow. This proves that an idiom is not a frozen quotation; it can live in a real sentence while its internal image remains fixed.

These idioms describe three very different everyday problems: fainting, being fooled, and having too much work. Their literal images are nonsense; their grammar and register are not.

> *Elle est tombée dans les pommes dans le métro.* — She fainted on the metro.

*Tomber dans les pommes* uses *être* in the passé composé because the person falls: *elle est tombée*. It is an informal everyday alternative to the more clinical *s'évanouir*.

> *Je me suis fait rouler dans la farine.* — I got conned.

This passive-like construction means someone deceived you. Name the deceiver with *par*: *Il s'est fait rouler dans la farine par un faux vendeur*. It suggests manipulation or a trick, not just a minor misunderstanding.

> *On a du pain sur la planche.* — We have a lot of work ahead of us.

This is a useful workplace-friendly idiom. It means there is a substantial workload, not that bread is literally waiting on a wooden board. It is less sharp than an insult and works well among colleagues.

## Quick check

- fainted: *est tombé(e) dans les pommes*
- was duped: *s'est fait rouler dans la farine*
- has plenty to do: *a du pain sur la planche*

Use the first two informally. The last is broadly safe, though a formal report still benefits from the plain *nous avons beaucoup de travail*.

The safest learning strategy is to recognise the colourful expression first, then test it in a low-risk conversation. If an idiom feels too theatrical for your relationship with the listener, a neutral paraphrase always sounds more natural than a phrase used with the wrong tone.
