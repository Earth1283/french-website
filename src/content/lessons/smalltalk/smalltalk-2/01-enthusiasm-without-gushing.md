# Enthusiasm Without Gushing

Small talk about opinions is where a learner can sound either warm or ridiculous. The safe middle is easy to reach: say what you like, say what you do not like, and keep it short. A plain *C'est magnifique* from you will land well, because it is specific and sincere. Ten superlatives in a row will not.

## The core expressions

> *C'est magnifique !* — It's magnificent!
>
> *C'est incroyable !* — It's incredible!
>
> *J'adore ça !* — I love that!
>
> *Je suis content(e).* — I'm happy.
>
> *Vous êtes très sympa.* — You're very kind.
>
> *Je suis fatigué(e).* — I'm tired.
>
> *Je n'aime pas trop ça.* — I don't really like that.

Notice how each one has a clear target. You are reacting to a view, a meal, a host or your own state of mind. Vague enthusiasm sounds like noise; a specific reaction sounds like a person.

## Restraint is the local style

French conversation tolerates understatement well. A host who has cooked for hours may respond to *C'est très bon* with more warmth than a torrent of praise would get. The aim is credibility, not volume. *Magnifique* is a word you can spend once or twice an evening.

## Why the negative is useful

Learning to say *Je n'aime pas trop ça* is more valuable than learning another superlative. It lets you refuse a dish, decline an activity or disagree with an opinion without sounding rude. It is a soft door, and most French speakers will walk through it with you.
