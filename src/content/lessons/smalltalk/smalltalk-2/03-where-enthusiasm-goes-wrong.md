# Where Enthusiasm Goes Wrong

Most of the mistakes in this lesson are false friends or register slips. Each one is harmless once you know it, and embarrassing if you do not.

## *Content* is not "content"

In English, "content" means satisfied. In French, *content* means happy or pleased. If you are mildly satisfied with a dinner, *je suis content* is an overstatement, though not a disaster. For a calmer feeling, *satisfait* is the precise word.

## *Excité* is risky

*Je suis excité* can mean "I'm eager," but in many contexts it sounds sexual or simply jittery. A safer way to show polite delight is *Je suis ravi(e)*, which means "delighted." It has the warmth of the English word without the ambiguity.

## *J'en ai marre* is not for hosts

*J'en ai marre* means "I'm fed up," and it is perfectly normal among friends. Saying it at a dinner party, even when you are exhausted, turns a pleasant evening into a complaint. Say *Je suis fatigué(e)* instead.

## *Tu* and *vous*

*Vous êtes très sympa* suits a host, a shopkeeper or someone older than you. *Tu es très sympa* is for friends. Mixing them up is more noticeable than any single adjective.

## *Froid* is not a personality

*Je suis froid* describes someone unfriendly. If you want to say you are cold, use *j'ai froid*, which is about your body and nothing else.

## Overusing the big words

*Incroyable* and *magnifique* lose their force when every meal is *incroyable*. Save the strong words for things that deserve them, and let *c'est bon* or *c'est très bien* do the everyday work.
