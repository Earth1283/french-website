# The Impersonal *Il* and the Verbs of Weather

English says "it is raining" and "it is cold," and French does the same thing with *il*. This *il* is a dummy subject. It points to nothing in particular, and it is the reason weather sentences look a little odd when you first meet them.

## *Faire* for temperature and sky

Most weather sentences use the verb *faire* with an adjective: *il fait beau*, *il fait mauvais*, *il fait chaud*, *il fait froid*. Notice there is no article. You say *il fait froid*, not *il fait le froid*. The adjective is almost always masculine singular, because *il* does not refer to a noun that could give it a gender. It is *il fait mauvais* even on a day you would call *une journée mauvaise*.

## *Pleuvoir* and *neiger* stand alone

Two verbs do not need *faire*. *Pleuvoir* means "to rain," and it is conjugated only in the third person: *il pleut*. *Neiger* means "to snow," and it is also used with *il*: *il neige*. The *e* after the *g* keeps the consonant soft, so the spelling is *neige* and the sound is *nɛʒ*, with the *g* like the *s* in "measure."

## Talking about the future

To say it is going to rain, use the near future: *il va pleuvoir*. Add *demain* or *cet après-midi* to place it. The structure is the same as English "it's going to rain," so it is one of the few weather structures that translates easily.

## Pronunciation notes

*Il fait* is pronounced *il fɛ*, with the *t* silent. *Il pleut* sounds like *il plø*. The subject *il* stays light, and the verb carries the stress.
