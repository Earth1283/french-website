# Traps With Temperature and Time

The weather vocabulary is short, which makes it tempting to assume the grammar is simple too. It is simple, but English speakers still fall into a few predictable traps. Each one is easy to fix once you can name it.

## *Avoir froid*, not *être froid*

English "I'm cold" describes your body, and French uses *avoir*: *j'ai froid*, *j'ai chaud*. *Je suis froid* describes someone with a cold manner, which is a rather unflattering thing to tell a host. Keep the feeling verb with *avoir* and the weather verb with *faire*.

## *Je suis chaud* is not a temperature

Learners sometimes say *je suis chaud* to mean "I'm hot." In slang, *être chaud* can mean eager or, in a different context, something more suggestive. Stick to *j'ai chaud* for body heat, and avoid any slang you have not heard used in context.

## *Temps* means both weather and time

*Quel temps fait-il ?* asks about the weather. *Je n'ai pas le temps* means "I don't have time." Both use *temps*, and the sentence around the word tells you which one is meant. In a shop, a question about *le temps* is almost always about the sky.

## *Mauvais*, not *mal*

*Mauvais* is an adjective, so it works after *il fait*. *Mal* is an adverb, and it describes how something is done or how you feel physically. You will not say *il fait mal* for bad weather, though *j'ai mal* means "I am in pain" and is useful to know.

## Thinking in English structure

Do not translate "it is cold" as *il est froid*. The French structure is *il fait froid*. The rule is simple: temperature and sky use *faire*, and your own sensations use *avoir*.
