# Hear Numbers Inside Prices, Ages, and Addresses

Knowing a number in isolation does not guarantee you will recognize it in a sentence. Real speech adds currency, nouns, liaisons, and surrounding information. Practice the frames in which numbers actually appear.

## Prices

French prices use *euro* and *centime*:

> *Ça coûte vingt-deux euros.* — It costs €22.
>
> *C'est quatre-vingt-dix-neuf euros.* — It is €99.
>
> *Trente euros cinquante.* — €30.50.

In quick speech, a cashier may omit *euros* between the whole number and cents. Context tells you that *douze cinquante* means €12.50, not 12 plus 50.

## Age

French uses *avoir* (“to have”), not *être*, for age:

> *J'ai trente et un ans.* — I am 31 years old.
>
> *Elle a soixante-douze ans.* — She is 72 years old.

Do not say *je suis trente et un*. The final *s* of *ans* is normally silent, while a linking consonant may connect the number to *ans*.

## Addresses, floors, and phone numbers

> *J'habite au quatre-vingt-deux, rue Nationale.* — I live at 82 National Street.
>
> *C'est au troisième étage.* — It is on the third floor.

French phone numbers are commonly grouped into pairs when spoken: 06 24 71 38 90 can be read *zéro six, vingt-quatre, soixante et onze, trente-huit, quatre-vingt-dix*. That makes mastery of 0–99 especially practical.

## Ask for confirmation

If one number matters, repeat it:

> *Soixante-douze ?* — Seventy-two?
>
> *Vous avez dit quatre-vingt-dix ?* — Did you say ninety?
>
> *Vous pouvez l'écrire ?* — Can you write it?

Never be embarrassed to verify a price, platform, address, or phone number. Native speakers also confirm numbers when audio is poor or consequences matter.
