# A Reliable Method for Decoding Large French Numbers

When a French number arrives quickly, identify its largest chunk first. Do not translate every syllable into English.

## Step 1: catch the opening

- *vingt…* → 20s
- *trente…* → 30s
- *quarante…* → 40s
- *cinquante…* → 50s
- *soixante…* → 60s or 70s
- *quatre-vingt…* → 80s or 90s

## Step 2: interpret the ending

After *soixante*, an ending from 1–9 gives 61–69; an ending from 10–19 gives 70–79. After *quatre-vingt*, an ending from 1–9 gives 81–89; an ending from 10–19 gives 90–99.

Examples:

- *soixante-quatre*: 60 + 4 = 64
- *soixante-quatorze*: 60 + 14 = 74
- *quatre-vingt-six*: 80 + 6 = 86
- *quatre-vingt-seize*: 80 + 16 = 96

The endings *quatre* and *quatorze*, or *six* and *seize*, are easy to confuse. Listen for the extra syllable: *quatorze* and *seize* are longer.

Cover one side and convert:

| Digits | French |

| 41 | *quarante et un* |
| 68 | *soixante-huit* |

| 80 | *quatre-vingts* |
| 81 | *quatre-vingt-un* |
| 92 | *quatre-vingt-douze* |
| 99 | *quatre-vingt-dix-neuf* |

## Use production, not recognition alone

Choose five numbers connected to your life: your age, building number, a price, part of a phone number, and a year. Say each in a full sentence. Then ask someone or text-to-speech software to read random numbers while you write the digits.

The irregular system becomes automatic through chunk recognition. You should eventually hear *quatre-vingt-quatorze* as one value—94—not as a math problem you solve consciously.
