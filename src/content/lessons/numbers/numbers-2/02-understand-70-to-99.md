# Understand the Logic of 70–99

Standard French in France changes strategy after 69. Instead of inventing new base-ten words, it combines 60 and 20 with smaller numbers. The arithmetic looks strange, but the construction is consistent.

## The seventies: 60 + 10

Seventy is *soixante-dix*—literally sixty-ten. Continue by adding the numbers 11 through 19:

| Number | Construction | French |
|---:|---|---|
| 70 | 60 + 10 | *soixante-dix* |
| 71 | 60 + 11 | *soixante et onze* |
| 72 | 60 + 12 | *soixante-douze* |
| 75 | 60 + 15 | *soixante-quinze* |
| 79 | 60 + 19 | *soixante-dix-neuf* |

Seventy-one keeps *et*: *soixante et onze*. This is a special form worth memorizing as one chunk.

## The eighties: four twenties

Eighty is *quatre-vingts*, literally “four twenties.” The final *s* appears when 80 stands alone. When another number follows, it disappears:

- 80: *quatre-vingts*
- 81: *quatre-vingt-un*
- 82: *quatre-vingt-deux*

There is no *et* in 81. Say *quatre-vingt-un*, not *quatre-vingt et un*.

## The nineties: 80 + 10

Ninety is *quatre-vingt-dix*. Continue from there:

- 91: *quatre-vingt-onze*
- 94: *quatre-vingt-quatorze*
- 99: *quatre-vingt-dix-neuf*

When listening, do not perform a written calculation after every word. Train the opening chunks as instant values: *soixante-dix* signals the seventies; *quatre-vingt* signals 80 or above. Then listen to the ending for the precise number.

Some French-speaking regions use forms such as *septante* for 70 and *nonante* for 90. These are valid regional forms. The system above is the one you need to understand in France and is widely understood across the French-speaking world.
