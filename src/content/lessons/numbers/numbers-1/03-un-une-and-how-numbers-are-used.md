# *Un* or *Une*, and How Numbers Behave in Sentences

Numbers do not act alone in French. They sit inside sentences, and the most common mistake is forgetting that *un* changes its form to agree with the noun. The second is treating *vingt* as if it ends in a sound you can hear.

## The agreement rule

> *un homme* — one man
>
> *une femme* — one woman
>
> *un euro* — one euro
>
> *une chambre* — one room

*Un* is masculine and *une* is feminine. Any noun that is grammatically masculine takes *un*, and any feminine noun takes *une*. Learn the gender of the noun at the same time, and the number follows.

## Numbers before a noun

When you state a quantity, the number stays the same but the noun takes its usual form. *Deux* is followed directly by the noun: *deux chambres*, *trois personnes*. *Personne* is feminine, so one person is *une personne*, and two people are *deux personnes*.

## Number plus unit

Counting money, time, or people is one of the first real uses of these numbers. *Cinq euros* is pronounced /sɛ̃k øʁo/, with the plural *s* silent. Even though the spelling shows *euros*, you do not pronounce that *s*. This is the most common listening surprise in French prices.

## A short exchange

> **Shop:** *Ça fait onze euros.*
>
> **You:** *Voilà. Vous avez la monnaie de vingt ?*
>
> **Shop:** *Oui, voilà.*

*Ça fait* is the usual way to state a total. *Vingt* here means a twenty-euro note, and the question *Vous avez la monnaie de vingt ?* asks whether they can give change for it.

## The main point

Numbers are the building blocks of prices, dates, and orders. Once you know which number to use, the next task is making the grammar match the noun, and that is a matter of noticing gender and plural forms as you go.
