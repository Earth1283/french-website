# The Numbers One to Ten

Prices, room numbers, phone digits, and the size of your party all depend on the same small group of words. Learn the numbers from one to ten first. Almost everything else in the early counting builds on them.

## The first ten

| French | Number | French | Number |
|---|---|---|---|
| *un / une* | 1 | *six* | 6 |
| *deux* | 2 | *sept* | 7 |
| *trois* | 3 | *huit* | 8 |
| *quatre* | 4 | *neuf* | 9 |
| *cinq* | 5 | *dix* | 10 |

Most of these words are short and easy to say. Their pronunciation, though, is where many learners stumble. *Huit* is pronounced /ɥit/, with a sound close to a "w" at the start, not "wit." *Neuf* ends with a clear /f/. *Six* is /sis/ on its own.

## One: *un* or *une*

*Un* is masculine and *une* is feminine. You use the form that matches the noun that follows: *un café*, but *une bière*. When you count without a noun, you usually say *un*. The number and the article share the same spelling, so *un café* can mean "one coffee" or simply "a coffee," depending on the context.

## Silent letters in counting

Pronunciation varies with the word that follows. *Deux*, *trois* and *six* can change their final consonant sound before a vowel. Do not worry about this yet. The key is that the spelling is stable even when the sound changes, so write the numbers as they are shown.

## Use the numbers in context

> *Une table pour deux, s'il vous plaît.* — A table for two, please.
>
> *Je voudrais trois croissants.* — I'd like three croissants.

Count out loud with a friend or a shop receipt. Repetition is the only way these become automatic. Once you can say the first ten without pausing, the next group becomes much easier.
