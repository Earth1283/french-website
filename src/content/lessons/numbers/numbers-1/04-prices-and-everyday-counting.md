# Prices and Everyday Counting

The numbers from one to twenty show up most often in shops, cafés, and transport. This chapter puts them together in realistic moments, so you can recognize prices and answer simple quantity questions without thinking about each digit.

## Prices in euros

> *Un euro cinquante.* — One euro fifty.
>
> *Deux euros.* — Two euros.
>
> *Dix-neuf euros.* — Nineteen euros.

French prices are spoken as whole euros first, then cents. Note that *un euro cinquante* is written as 1,50 €. A café may say *Un café, c'est deux euros cinquante*, which is the same kind of count.

## Asking how much

> *Ça fait combien ?* — How much is that?
>
> *C'est combien ?* — How much is it?

Both are informal and useful. The answer comes as a number, so listen for the figure and repeat it back to confirm.

## Counting people and things

> *Nous sommes quatre.* — There are four of us.
>
> *Je voudrais six bouteilles d'eau.* — I'd like six bottles of water.

*Nous sommes quatre* is a common way to say how many people are in a group. You do not need the word *personnes* when the number is clear from context.

## A note on quantities

When asking for an amount, *un* and *une* are your basic forms. *Un* is also used for one item. You will see *une* before feminine nouns like *bouteille* or *table*, and *un* before masculine nouns like *café* or *billet*.

## Practice loop

Pick three prices from a menu or a shop window and read them aloud. Then say the number of items you want. Repeating real prices is more useful than rote drilling, because it attaches the numbers to things you actually buy.
