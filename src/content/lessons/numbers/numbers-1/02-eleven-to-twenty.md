# Eleven to Twenty: Irregular, But Learnable

The numbers from eleven to sixteen are not built from smaller parts in a predictable way. They are separate words, and you need to memorize them. From seventeen to nineteen, the logic becomes regular again, which is a relief.

## Eleven to sixteen

| French | Number |
|---|---|
| *onze* | 11 |
| *douze* | 12 |
| *treize* | 13 |
| *quatorze* | 14 |
| *quinze* | 15 |
| *seize* | 16 |

Notice how each word ends in *-ze*, which is a helpful pattern. *Onze* is pronounced /ɔ̃z/, *douze* /duz/, and *treize* /tʁɛz/. The *-ze* ending is audible in all of them, so listen for it.

## Seventeen to nineteen

> *dix-sept* — 17 (ten and seven)
>
> *dix-huit* — 18 (ten and eight)
>
> *dix-neuf* — 19 (ten and nine)

Here *dix* is combined with *sept*, *huit*, and *neuf*. These numbers are formed by adding the units to ten, and the hyphen marks the link. In speech, *dix-sept* is pronounced /dis sɛt/, and *dix-huit* is pronounced /diz ɥit/, with a *z* sound from the *x* before the vowel. This is the liaison you will hear, and it is normal.

## The number that loses its *t*

*Vingt*, twenty, is pronounced /vɛ̃/ on its own: the *g* and *t* are silent. The *t* comes back from 21 to 29 (*vingt-deux* is /vɛ̃t.dø/) and before a vowel (*vingt ans*), so the silence only lasts until the next number.

## Trap: *cinq* versus *quinze*

*Cinq* is five and *quinze* is fifteen. They look related, and they are, but the sounds differ. *Cinq* is pronounced /sɛ̃k/ with a hard *k* at the end, while *quinze* is /kɛ̃z/ and ends in a *z*. If you hear a *z* in a price, you are likely dealing with fifteen, not five. Check before you pay.

Test yourself by saying the number aloud, then writing it down. The written form is the one you will see on prices, room doors, and phone screens.
