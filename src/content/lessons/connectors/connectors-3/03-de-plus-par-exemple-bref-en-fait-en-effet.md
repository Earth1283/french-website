# De Plus, Par Exemple, Bref, En Fait, En Effet: Finishing the Toolkit

Five more small words round out your ability to add information, illustrate a point, summarize, and — most importantly — avoid one of the sneakiest look-alike traps in French.

## De plus / en plus: adding another point

Both mean "moreover" or "on top of that," stacking one more piece of information onto what was just said. *De plus* belongs to writing and formal speech; *en plus* is what people actually say out loud.

> *Le restaurant est excellent ; de plus, ce n'est pas cher.*
>
> The restaurant is excellent; moreover, it's not expensive.

*En plus* can also carry a hint of irritation in casual speech, stacking a complaint on top of another complaint:

> *Il est en retard, et en plus il n'a pas prévenu.*
>
> He's late, and on top of that he didn't warn us.

## Par exemple: illustrating a point

*Par exemple* means exactly what it looks like — "for example" — and slots in wherever you want to back up a general statement with a concrete case.

> *J'aime les sports d'hiver, par exemple le ski.*
>
> I like winter sports, for example skiing.

## Bref: wrapping it all up

*Bref* means "in short" or "anyway," and it signals that you're about to compress a longer story into its essential point, or simply move the conversation along.

> *Bref, on a raté le train.*
>
> In short, we missed the train.

It's a very spoken, very natural word — the kind of thing a friend says right before getting to the point after a long, winding explanation.

## En fait vs. en effet: siblings that don't get along

Here is the trap. *En fait* and *en effet* look like near-twins, and they are not interchangeable.

*En fait* means "actually" or "in fact," and it typically introduces a correction, a nuance, or a surprising truth that revises what came before.

> *Je pensais qu'il était absent, en fait il était là.*
>
> I thought he was absent, actually he was there.

*En effet* means "indeed" or "that's right," and it does the opposite job: it **confirms or agrees** with something that was just said, rather than correcting it.

> *— Il fait froid aujourd'hui. — En effet, il neige même.*
>
> — It's cold today. — Indeed, it's even snowing.

Swap them and the logic breaks: replying *En fait, il neige même* to "it's cold today" would strangely imply you're correcting the other person, when you actually agree and are simply adding more evidence. Use *en effet* to say "you're right, and here's proof"; use *en fait* to say "actually, here's what's really true."

## The full toolkit, together

> *Le voyage s'est bien passé. En fait, on a même eu de la chance avec la météo. Bref, on recommande cette destination — par exemple à des amis qui aiment la randonnée. En effet, les sentiers sont magnifiques.*
>
> The trip went well. Actually, we even got lucky with the weather. In short, we'd recommend this destination — for example to friends who like hiking. Indeed, the trails are gorgeous.
