# Puisque, Donc, and Alors: Shared Knowledge and Its Consequences

Not every reason is news to your listener. Sometimes you're pointing at something you both already know, and French has a word built exactly for that: *puisque*. Pair it with *donc* and *alors*, the two workhorses of consequence, and you can build a full cause-and-effect sentence that sounds like something a real person would say.

## Puisque: the reason we both already know

*Puisque* also means "since," but it carries a different flavor from *parce que*. Where *parce que* introduces new information, *puisque* treats the cause as obvious, already established, or already stated by someone in the conversation.

> *Puisque tu es là, aide-moi à porter les valises.*
>
> Since you're here, help me carry the suitcases.

Notice the tone: the speaker isn't informing you that you're there — you both already know that. *Puisque* leans on shared ground, which is why it can sound slightly pointed or impatient in the wrong context, close to "well, seeing as you're here anyway...". Compare that with *parce que*, which stays neutral no matter what.

> *Puisqu'il fait beau, sortons.*
>
> Since it's nice out, let's go outside.

## Donc: the logical "therefore"

*Donc* introduces a consequence that follows logically from what came before — it's the closest French gets to a mathematical "therefore." It's famous from Descartes' *Je pense, donc je suis* ("I think, therefore I am"), and that logical flavor persists in everyday use.

> *Il pleut, donc je prends un parapluie.*
>
> It's raining, so I'm taking an umbrella.

*Donc* works in both writing and speech, and it can also mean "so" in the sense of resuming a point: *Donc, comme je disais...* ("So, as I was saying...").

## Alors: the conversational "so, then"

*Alors* covers similar ground but leans much more casual and conversational. It often appears as a reaction in dialogue, introducing what should happen next given what was just said.

> *Tu es fatigué ? Alors repose-toi.*
>
> You're tired? Then rest.

*Alors* also does double duty as a simple time-sequence word meaning "then" (*d'abord..., alors...*), and as a filler that buys thinking time, much like English "so..." at the start of a sentence. In spoken French, *alors* often replaces *donc* entirely; *donc* holds onto more of its logical, slightly formal edge.

## Putting them together

> *Puisque tu es libre ce soir, on pourrait aller au cinéma, alors dis-moi si ça te tente.*
>
> Since you're free tonight, we could go to the movies — so tell me if you're up for it.

One sentence, two connectors, zero telegram style. That's the whole point.
