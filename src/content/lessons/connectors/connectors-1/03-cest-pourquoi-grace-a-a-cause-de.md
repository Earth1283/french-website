# C'est Pourquoi, Grâce À, and À Cause De: Naming the Result and Judging the Cause

Two more tools round out your cause-and-consequence kit: a phrase that flags a consequence in its own sentence, and a pair of prepositions that quietly judge whether a cause was good news or bad news.

## C'est pourquoi: a spotlight on the result

*C'est pourquoi* means "that's why" or "that's the reason," and it usually opens a new sentence or clause that states the consequence directly, after the cause has already been given.

> *Elle a raté son train, c'est pourquoi elle est en retard.*
>
> She missed her train, that's why she's late.

It's slightly more formal than *donc* and reads a little like a written explanation — the kind of phrase you'd find in an email justifying a decision, or a news report connecting an event to its outcome. Think of it as underlining the result: *here is the consequence, and here is why it happened.*

> *Le pont était fermé, c'est pourquoi nous avons pris un autre chemin.*
>
> The bridge was closed, that's why we took another route.

## Grâce à: the cause you're thankful for

*Grâce à* means "thanks to," and it only ever introduces a **positive** cause — something that helped, something you're grateful for.

> *J'ai réussi mon examen grâce à toi.*
>
> I passed my exam thanks to you.

Literally, *grâce* means "grace" or "favor," and that root sense never fully disappears: using *grâce à* frames the cause as a gift, a piece of good fortune.

## À cause de: the cause you're blaming

*À cause de* means "because of," and it leans the opposite way — toward a **negative or neutral** cause, something that caused a problem, a delay, or at best a plain fact with no positive spin.

> *Le vol est annulé à cause de la grève.*
>
> The flight is cancelled because of the strike.

Both *grâce à* and *à cause de* are followed by a noun or a pronoun, never directly by a full clause with its own conjugated verb — unlike *parce que*, *car*, *puisque*, and *comme*, which all introduce clauses.

## Don't cross the wires

This is the trap: French speakers instinctively feel the difference in connotation, and using the wrong one sounds either sarcastic or bizarre.

> *J'ai raté mon examen grâce à toi* would sound like a sarcastic jab — "thanks a lot" — even if you meant it neutrally.
>
> *J'ai réussi mon examen à cause de toi* sounds almost accusatory, as if your help were somehow a problem.

Match the word to the mood of the cause, and these two phrases will do real, precise work that English's all-purpose "because of" can't manage on its own.
