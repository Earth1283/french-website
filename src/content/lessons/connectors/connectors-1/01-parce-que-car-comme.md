# Parce Que, Car, and Comme: Three Words, One Trap

English speakers get lazy with "because" — it works everywhere, in any position, in any register. French refuses to let you do that. *Parce que*, *car*, and *comme* all translate as "because," but they sit in different places in the sentence and belong to different registers. Get the position wrong and a French listener will notice, even if they can't say why.

## Parce que: the default

*Parce que* is your neutral, everyday choice. It answers the question *pourquoi ?* directly and normally follows the result it explains.

> *Pourquoi tu pars ?* — *Parce que je suis fatigué.*
>
> Why are you leaving? — Because I'm tired.

You can even use *parce que* on its own, as a full answer to a question, the way English speakers say "because" as a complete (if grammatically incomplete) reply. If in doubt about which cause-word to reach for, *parce que* is almost always safe.

## Car: the formal cousin, never at the front

*Car* means the same thing but belongs to writing and careful, somewhat literary speech — the kind of French you'd find in a newspaper editorial or a formal letter. The crucial rule: **car never opens a sentence.** It joins two independent clauses, sitting right where the second one begins.

> *Le magasin a fermé plus tôt, car il n'y avait plus de clients.*
>
> The shop closed early, for there were no more customers.

You will never see "Car il n'y avait plus de clients" standing alone as its own sentence. If you want to lead with the reason, you need a different word entirely — which brings us to *comme*.

## Comme: cause first, always

*Comme* flips the whole structure. Instead of stating the result and then explaining it, *comme* puts the cause at the very front of the sentence and the result afterward. Think "as" or "since" in English when it opens a sentence: "As it was raining, we stayed in."

> *Comme il pleuvait, on est restés à la maison.*
>
> Since it was raining, we stayed home.

This word order isn't optional stylistic flair — it's the defining feature of *comme* as a cause-connector. Move the clauses around and swap in *parce que* instead: *On est restés à la maison parce qu'il pleuvait* works fine, but *"Il pleuvait, comme on est restés à la maison"* does not.

## The quick reference

- **Parce que** — neutral, flexible position, answers *pourquoi ?* directly.
- **Car** — formal/written, joins two clauses, never opens a sentence.
- **Comme** — always opens the sentence, cause before result.

Picture a courtroom: *parce que* is the witness answering a direct question, *car* is the lawyer adding a formal aside mid-argument, and *comme* is the judge setting the scene before anyone else speaks. Different jobs, different seats in the room.
