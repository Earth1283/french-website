# Mais, Pourtant, and Cependant: A Ladder of Contradiction

*Mais* is the first contrast word every learner meets, and it's genuinely useful — but leaning on it for every single contradiction makes your French sound flat, like a child's drawing done only in one color. *Pourtant* and *cependant* add shade and register to the same basic idea: "this happened, but something else, unexpectedly, also happened."

## Mais: the plain, all-purpose "but"

*Mais* is neutral and works everywhere, in speech and writing, for contrasts big and small.

> *Je voudrais venir, mais je suis fatigué.*
>
> I'd like to come, but I'm tired.

There's no drama in *mais* — it simply joins two facts that don't quite line up. It's the connector you reach for by default, the way you'd reach for "but" in English.

## Pourtant: the word for genuine surprise

*Pourtant* means "yet" or "and yet," and it's stronger than *mais*: it signals that the second fact genuinely contradicts what you would logically expect from the first.

> *Il a révisé, pourtant il a échoué.*
>
> He studied, and yet he failed.

Compare *Il a révisé, mais il a échoué* — grammatically fine, but flatter, almost a shrug. *Pourtant* adds the raised eyebrow: studying should have led to success, and it didn't. *Pourtant* often sits right at the start of the surprising clause, giving it extra punch.

> *Elle semblait calme ; pourtant, elle tremblait de peur.*
>
> She seemed calm; and yet she was trembling with fear.

## Cependant: pourtant's buttoned-up cousin

*Cependant* covers the same territory as *pourtant* — "however," "nevertheless" — but it belongs to a more formal, written register: essays, reports, the news, professional emails. You will rarely hear it tossed casually into a conversation between friends the way *pourtant* sometimes is.

> *Le prix a augmenté ; cependant, la qualité reste excellente.*
>
> The price has gone up; however, the quality remains excellent.

If you're writing a cover letter, a school essay, or an article, *cependant* signals that you know how to write formally. If you're chatting with a friend about a bad exam result, *pourtant* or plain *mais* will sound far more natural.

## Choosing between the three

- **Mais** — default, any register, no particular emphasis.
- **Pourtant** — stronger, signals genuine surprise or contradiction, works in speech and writing.
- **Cependant** — same meaning as pourtant, but reserved for formal or written French.

Think of it as a volume knob: *mais* is a normal speaking voice, *pourtant* raises an eyebrow, and *cependant* puts on a suit before entering the room. All three say "but" — they just dress differently for the occasion.
