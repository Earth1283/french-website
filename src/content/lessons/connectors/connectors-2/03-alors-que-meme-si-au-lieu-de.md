# Alors Que, Même Si, and Au Lieu De: Contrast Beyond "But"

Not every contrast fits neatly into a "but" sentence. Sometimes you need to compare two situations side by side, concede a hypothetical without weakening your point, or swap one action for another. Three more structures handle those jobs precisely.

## Alors que and tandis que: whereas

*Alors que* and *tandis que* both mean "whereas" or "while," setting two facts side by side for comparison rather than outright contradiction. Both take the indicative mood, just like an ordinary statement of fact.

> *Il fait chaud à Nice, alors qu'il neige à Grenoble.*
>
> It's warm in Nice, whereas it's snowing in Grenoble.

> *Elle travaille le week-end, tandis que son frère se repose.*
>
> She works on weekends, whereas her brother rests.

The two are close to interchangeable; *tandis que* leans slightly more literary, but you'll hear both in everyday speech. Neither one requires a subjunctive verb — the comparison is presented as plain fact, not doubt or emotion.

## Même si: even if, but still indicative

*Même si* means "even if" or "even though," and it introduces a concession — you're acknowledging a condition without letting it change your conclusion.

> *Même si tu es fatigué, viens à la fête.*
>
> Even if you're tired, come to the party.

Here is the trap that catches almost every intermediate learner: *même si* **looks** hypothetical, the way *bien que* ("although") or *pour que* ("so that") do — and those words really do trigger the subjunctive. *Même si* does not. It behaves like *si* in a regular conditional sentence and stays firmly in the indicative.

> Correct: *Même si tu es fatigué, viens.*
>
> Incorrect: ~~*Même si tu sois fatigué, viens.*~~

Memorize this one exception and you'll avoid one of the most common subjunctive mistakes English speakers make.

## Au lieu de: instead of doing something

*Au lieu de* means "instead of," and it's followed by an infinitive when the same person is doing both the skipped action and the chosen one.

> *Il a pris le bus au lieu de marcher.*
>
> He took the bus instead of walking.

The subject of the sentence (*il*) is the one who could have walked and instead took the bus — one subject, two competing actions, infinitive after *au lieu de*. If the two actions belong to different people, French technically switches to *au lieu que* plus the subjunctive (*au lieu que je marche*), but that construction is rare and sounds stiff; most speakers simply rephrase the sentence with two separate clauses instead of forcing it.

## Quick contrast recap

- **Alors que / tandis que** — side-by-side comparison, indicative.
- **Même si** — concession, looks hypothetical, stays indicative.
- **Au lieu de** + infinitive — same subject, swapped action.

Three structures, three different jobs — none of them just "mais" in disguise.
