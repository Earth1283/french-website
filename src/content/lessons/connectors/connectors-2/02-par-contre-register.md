# Par Contre: The Word Grammarians Love to Hate (and Everyone Uses Anyway)

Few French connectors carry as much baggage as *par contre*. If you've studied French from an older textbook, or spoken to a certain kind of purist, you may have been told to avoid it entirely. Here is the actual, current truth: **par contre is completely standard French**, used constantly by educated native speakers in speech and in the overwhelming majority of everyday writing.

## What it means

*Par contre* means "but" or "on the other hand," functioning almost exactly like *mais*, though it typically introduces a contrast that balances a positive with a negative, or vice versa.

> *J'adore la cuisine française, par contre je déteste faire la vaisselle.*
>
> I love French cuisine, but I hate doing the dishes.

> *Le film était long, par contre les acteurs étaient excellents.*
>
> The film was long, but the actors were excellent.

Note the shape of both examples: one clause states something, the second clause pivots to the opposite quality. That balancing act is where *par contre* feels most natural.

## Where the myth comes from

In the early twentieth century, a handful of influential grammarians — most famously in a widely cited but disputed remark attributed to André Gide — argued that *par contre* was clumsy or illogical and that *en revanche* should be used instead. That opinion got repeated in classrooms for decades, long after actual usage moved on. Today, the *Académie française* itself has softened its historical objection, and *par contre* appears constantly in newspapers, novels, and professional writing, not just casual speech.

## When to still reach for en revanche

*En revanche* means the same thing and remains the safer choice in two situations:

- A very formal or literary piece of writing, where a conservative reader might still raise an eyebrow at *par contre*.
- An exam or a teacher who explicitly still enforces the old rule (some French classes, especially exam-prep courses, still do).

> *Le taux de chômage a baissé ; en revanche, l'inflation reste élevée.*
>
> Unemployment has dropped; however, inflation remains high.

Outside of those specific contexts, *par contre* and *en revanche* are interchangeable, and *par contre* is, if anything, the more common of the two in real conversation.

## The takeaway

Don't let outdated advice make you self-conscious about a word that literally every French person uses. Learn *par contre* as your default "but, on the other hand," and keep *en revanche* in reserve for moments when the room calls for a jacket and tie.
