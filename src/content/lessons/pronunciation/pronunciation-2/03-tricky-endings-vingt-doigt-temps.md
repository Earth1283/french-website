# Tricky Endings: *vingt*, *doigt*, *temps*

Some French words carry a heavy tail of silent letters. They look frightening on paper and are easy in speech once you know the shape. This chapter covers the ones that trip up learners most often.

## Words with two or three silent letters

| Written | Sounds like | Why it looks hard |
|---|---|---|
| *vingt* (20) | vahn (nasal vowel) | *g* and *t* are silent |
| *doigt* (finger) | dwah | *g* and *t* silent |
| *temps* (time, weather) | tahn (nasal) | *p* and *s* silent |
| *corps* (body) | kor | *p* and *s* silent |
| *poids* (weight) | pwah | *d* and *s* silent |
| *champs* (fields) | shahn | *p* and *s* silent |

Take them in pieces. Say the **vowel sound first**, then ignore every letter after it. *Temps* is a nasal vowel with a *t* in front. That is all there is to it.

## *Vingt* changes in company

Alone, *vingt* ends in a nasal vowel: *vahn*. But in 21 to 29 the *t* is pronounced, and you hear it:

- *vingt* → vahn
- *vingt et un* (21) → van-tay-UN
- *vingt-deux* (22) → vant-DEUH

Numbers often behave differently inside a sequence, and you will meet more of this in the numbers unit. For now, remember that *vingt* on its own is silent-ending.

## Numbers that break the rule

Several numbers go against the silent-consonant default. They are everyday words:

- *sept* (7) — pronounced *set*. The *p* is silent, the *t* is not.
- *huit* (8) — pronounced *weet*. The final *t* is heard.
- *cinq* (5) — pronounced *sank*. The *q* is heard.
- *neuf* (9) — pronounced *nuhf*. The *f* is heard.

*Six* and *dix* depend on what comes next: before a consonant the *x* is silent (*six chaises*), before a vowel it links as a *z* (*six ans*), and at the end of a phrase it is pronounced (*j'en ai six*).

## Two spellings, one word

The same letters can be pronounced or silent depending on the word:

- *fils* (son) is pronounced *fiss*, with the *s* heard.
- *tous* as a pronoun is pronounced *tooss*, as an adjective (*tous les jours*) *too*.
- *plus* can be *plü* or *plüss* depending on meaning.

When a word looks inconsistent, it is almost always a real irregularity, not your mistake. Learn these as exceptions, with the pronunciation attached to the word.
