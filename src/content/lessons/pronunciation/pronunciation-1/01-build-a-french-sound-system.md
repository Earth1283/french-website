# Stop Reading French as English

French pronunciation becomes much easier when you stop asking, “What sound would these letters make in English?” French spelling is not random, but it represents a different sound system. Your first job is therefore not to perfect an accent. It is to build a few new sound categories so that words which look similar do not collapse into the same English approximation.

## Three habits that matter immediately

First, keep vowels **short and steady**. English vowels often glide: the vowel in “day” begins in one place and moves toward an *ee* sound. French *é*, as in *café*, stays in one place. Say a clean *eh* and freeze the mouth position.

Second, do not give every written consonant equal weight. French syllables connect smoothly, and many final consonants are silent. *Paris* ends in an *ee* sound, not an English *s*. You will study the detailed rules later; for now, resist the urge to pronounce every letter you see.

Third, learn with your body. A pronunciation description is useful only if it tells your tongue, lips, jaw, and airflow what to do. When a sound is difficult, exaggerate the mouth position slowly, then shorten it. Speed comes after control.

## The minimum useful sound map

This lesson concentrates on five sound families:

- ***r* /ʁ/**: made toward the back of the mouth, not with the English tongue shape
- ***u* /y/**: the tongue position of *ee* combined with rounded lips
- ***eu* /ø/**: rounded like *u*, but with a lower, more relaxed tongue
- ***é* /e/ and *è* /ɛ/**: two distinct “e” sounds
- **nasal vowels**, such as *on* /ɔ̃/: air resonates through the nose while the following *n* is usually not released as a separate consonant

You do not need to memorize the IPA symbols to learn French. They are included as precise labels, like map coordinates. The physical instructions and example words are what you should practice.

## What “good enough” sounds like

A useful accent is one that keeps meanings apart and lets listeners recognize words without guessing. Your *r* does not need to sound Parisian on day one. Your *u* and *ou*, however, should not be identical, because *tu* (“you”) and *tout* (“everything”) are different words. Aim first for clear contrasts, then polish the accent over time.
