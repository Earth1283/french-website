# Build a Negative Sentence Around the Verb

French standard negation uses two parts around the conjugated verb: *ne* before it and *pas* after it.

> *Je parle français.* → *Je ne parle pas français.*
>
> *Elle est prête.* → *Elle n'est pas prête.*
>
> *Nous avons réservé.* → *Nous n'avons pas réservé.*

Before a vowel or silent *h*, *ne* contracts to *n'*: *je n'ai pas*, *il n'habite pas ici*. The verb stays in the middle of the “negative sandwich.”

## Spoken French often drops *ne*

In everyday conversation you will frequently hear:

> *Je sais pas.* — I don't know.
>
> *On a pas le temps.* — We don't have time.

The meaning is negative because *pas* remains. Learn and write the full *ne…pas* pattern first; recognize the shortened form so real speech does not surprise you. In formal writing and careful speech, keep *ne*.

## Articles after negation

With *avoir* and many ordinary verbs, *un, une, du, de la,* and *des* generally become *de / d'* after negation:

> *J'ai une voiture.* → *Je n'ai pas de voiture.*
>
> *Il boit du café.* → *Il ne boit pas de café.*
>
> *Nous avons des enfants.* → *Nous n'avons pas d'enfants.*

Definite articles remain because they identify something specific: *Je n'aime pas le café* (“I do not like coffee”). With *être*, the article also remains: *Ce n'est pas un problème*.

## More precise negatives

Replace *pas* with another negative word:

- *ne…jamais* — never: *Je ne fume jamais.*
- *ne…plus* — no longer / no more: *Il ne travaille plus ici.*
- *ne…rien* — nothing: *Je ne comprends rien.*
- *ne…personne* — nobody: *Je ne connais personne.*

These follow the same core logic: the conjugated verb sits after *ne*, while the negative meaning is completed by the second element.
