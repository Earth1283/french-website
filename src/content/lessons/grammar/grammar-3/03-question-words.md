# Ask for the Missing Information

A yes-or-no question checks a whole idea. A question word asks for one missing piece. Put the question word before *est-ce que* for a reliable neutral structure.

| French | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *où* | where | *Où est-ce que vous habitez ?* |
| *quand* | when | *Quand est-ce que le train part ?* |
| *pourquoi* | why | *Pourquoi est-ce que vous apprenez le français ?* |
| *comment* | how / what…like | *Comment est-ce que ça marche ?* |
| *combien* | how much / many | *Combien est-ce que ça coûte ?* |
| *qui* | who | *Qui est-ce que vous attendez ?* |

## *Que* becomes *qu'est-ce que*

To ask “what” as the object of a sentence, use *qu'est-ce que*:

> *Qu'est-ce que vous voulez ?* — What do you want?
>
> *Qu'est-ce que tu fais ?* — What are you doing?

To identify something, the fixed expression is *Qu'est-ce que c'est ?* (“What is it?”). In casual speech, you may hear *C'est quoi ?* It is common but more informal.

## Short spoken forms

Conversation often moves the question word to the end:

> *Vous habitez où ?* — Where do you live?
>
> *Le train part quand ?* — When does the train leave?
>
> *Ça coûte combien ?* — How much does that cost?

These are natural spoken questions. The fronted forms are clearer for careful speech and writing.

## *Quel* agrees with a noun

*Quel* means “which” or “what” before a noun and changes form:

- *quel train* — which train
- *quelle heure* — what time
- *quels jours* — which days
- *quelles stations* — which stations

Learn *Quelle heure est-il ?* as a complete phrase. The feminine form *quelle* agrees with *heure*.
