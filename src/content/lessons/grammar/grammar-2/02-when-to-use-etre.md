# Use *Être* for Identity, Description, and State

*Être* connects a subject to what that subject is. The complement may identify, describe, locate, or express a condition.

## Identity and profession

> *Je suis Léa.* — I am Léa.
>
> *Nous sommes étudiants.* — We are students.
>
> *Il est médecin.* — He is a doctor.

French normally uses no indefinite article for a profession directly after *être*: *elle est ingénieure*, not *elle est une ingénieure*. An article returns when the profession is modified: *C'est une excellente ingénieure* (“She is an excellent engineer”).

## Nationality and description

> *Je suis français / française.* — I am French.
>
> *Elles sont fatiguées.* — They are tired.
>
> *Vous êtes très gentil / gentille.* — You are very kind.

Adjectives agree with the noun or person they describe. A feminine form often adds written *-e*, and a plural often adds *-s*. Some changes affect pronunciation; others do not. Store the common forms with examples rather than relying only on spelling rules.

## State and location

> *Tu es prêt ?* — Are you ready?
>
> *Le café est fermé.* — The café is closed.
>
> *Nous sommes à Paris.* — We are in Paris.

Unlike Spanish, French does not divide “to be” between permanent and temporary states. *Être* covers both identity and condition.

## *Il est* versus *c'est*

Use *c'est* to identify or present something: *C'est Marie* (“That/It is Marie”), *C'est un musée* (“It is a museum”). Use *il est / elle est* when the person or thing is already identified and you describe it: *Marie ? Elle est architecte. Elle est sympa.*

A practical beginner rule is: *c'est + noun with article/name*; *il/elle est + adjective or unmodified profession*. Real French has more nuance, but this distinction produces natural sentences immediately.
