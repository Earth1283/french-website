# Use *Avoir* Where English Often Uses “Be”

*Avoir* expresses possession, but French also uses it in many everyday states that English builds with “to be.” These expressions must be learned as French patterns, not translated word by word.

## Possession

> *J'ai un billet.* — I have a ticket.
>
> *Vous avez une réservation ?* — Do you have a reservation?
>
> *Ils ont deux enfants.* — They have two children.

The forms *ils ont* and *ils sont* are easy to confuse. *Ont* belongs to *avoir* (“have”); *sont* belongs to *être* (“are”). The liaison also differs: *ils ont* is pronounced with a linking /z/.

## Physical states with *avoir*

| French expression | Literal structure | Natural English |
|---|---|---|
| *avoir faim* | to have hunger | to be hungry |
| *avoir soif* | to have thirst | to be thirsty |
| *avoir chaud / froid* | to have heat / cold | to be hot / cold |
| *avoir peur* | to have fear | to be afraid |
| *avoir sommeil* | to have sleepiness | to be sleepy |
| *avoir mal à…* | to have pain in… | to hurt / have an ache |

So “I am hungry” is *j'ai faim*, not *je suis faim*. “My head hurts” is *j'ai mal à la tête*.

## Age with *avoir*

> *J'ai vingt ans.* — I am twenty years old.
>
> *Quel âge avez-vous ?* — How old are you?
>
> *Elle a huit ans.* — She is eight years old.

Again, French conceptualizes age as something you have. *Je suis vingt ans* is not grammatical.

## Need and luck

Two more high-frequency chunks are *avoir besoin de* (“to need”) and *avoir de la chance* (“to be lucky”):

> *Nous avons besoin d'aide.* — We need help.
>
> *Tu as de la chance !* — You are lucky!

The best strategy is to memorize the entire expression beginning with *avoir*. That prevents English from choosing the wrong French verb for you.
