# What Conjugation Actually Does

A French verb changes form to show who performs the action. English does this only a little—“I am,” “you are,” “she is”—but French does it for every verb. The subject pronoun and verb form belong together as a pair.

## The subject pronouns

| Pronoun | Basic meaning |
|---|---|
| *je* | I |
| *tu* | you, one familiar person |
| *il / elle / on* | he / she / one, we in everyday speech |
| *nous* | we |
| *vous* | you formally, or more than one person |
| *ils / elles* | they, mixed/masculine group / all-feminine group |

*On* deserves special attention. It literally can mean “one” or “people,” but in ordinary conversation it very often means “we”: *On est prêts ?* (“Are we ready?”). It takes the same verb form as *il* and *elle*, not the *nous* form.

## Why *être* and *avoir* must be learned early

*Être* (“to be”) and *avoir* (“to have”) are irregular, extremely frequent, and used to build other grammatical forms. Memorize them in rhythm:

| Subject | *être* | *avoir* |
|---|---|---|
| *je* | *suis* | *j'ai* |
| *tu* | *es* | *as* |
| *il / elle / on* | *est* | *a* |
| *nous* | *sommes* | *avons* |
| *vous* | *êtes* | *avez* |
| *ils / elles* | *sont* | *ont* |

Before a vowel, *je* becomes *j'*, so it is *j'ai*, never *je ai*. Several written endings are silent: *tu es* and *il est* share the same vowel sound, while the pronoun tells you who is meant.

Learn full pairs—*nous sommes*, *vous avez*—instead of detached endings. This makes retrieval faster and prevents mismatches such as *vous sommes*.
