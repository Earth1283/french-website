# Build Sentences with *Être* and *Avoir*

Use a three-part frame: **subject + conjugated verb + complement**. The complement carries the new information.

> *Je + suis + fatigué.*
>
> *Vous + avez + une réservation.*
>
> *Nous + sommes + à Lyon.*

## Make the sentence negative

Place *ne* before the conjugated verb and *pas* after it:

> *Je ne suis pas malade.* — I am not sick.
>
> *Nous n'avons pas de voiture.* — We do not have a car.

Before a vowel, *ne* becomes *n'*. Notice another change: after a negative form of *avoir*, an indefinite article often becomes *de*: *j'ai une voiture → je n'ai pas de voiture*.

## Make the sentence a question

The easiest neutral method is *est-ce que* + statement order:

> *Est-ce que vous êtes prêts ?* — Are you ready?
>
> *Est-ce qu'elle a un billet ?* — Does she have a ticket?

In everyday speech, rising intonation is also common: *Vous avez une réservation ?* Formal writing may use inversion: *Avez-vous une réservation ?*

## Choose the correct verb

Complete these mentally before revealing the answer:

1. I am American. → *Je ___ américain(e).* → ***suis***
2. We are hungry. → *Nous ___ faim.* → ***avons***
3. She is in Paris. → *Elle ___ à Paris.* → ***est***
4. They have two tickets. → *Ils ___ deux billets.* → ***ont***
5. You are cold. → *Tu ___ froid.* → ***as***

The English word “are” in item 2 does not determine the French verb; the French expression *avoir faim* does.

## A useful self-check

For every sentence, ask:

1. Who is the subject?
2. Which idea requires *être* or *avoir* in French?
3. Does the verb form match the subject?
4. Does an adjective need agreement?

That checklist turns conjugation from guesswork into a repeatable decision.
