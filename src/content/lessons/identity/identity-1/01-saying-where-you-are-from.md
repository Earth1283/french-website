# Saying Where You Are From

The most useful sentence in this lesson is *Je viens de…* ("I come from…"). It looks simple until you add a country, and then French asks a question English never does: is that country masculine, feminine, or plural?

## The basic frame

> *Je viens de Paris.* — I come from Paris.
>
> *Je viens de Londres.* — I come from London.
>
> *Je suis de Lyon.* — I'm from Lyon.

With **cities**, you use *de* (or *d'* before a vowel: *d'Amsterdam*) and nothing else. That is the easy case.

## Countries have gender

With countries, the preposition depends on gender and number:

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| feminine country | *de / d'* | *Je viens de France, d'Australie, d'Italie* |
| masculine country | *du* | *Je viens du Canada, du Japon, du Brésil* |
| plural country | *des* | *Je viens des États-Unis, des Pays-Bas* |

**How to tell gender:** countries ending in **-e** are almost always feminine (*la France, l'Italie, l'Allemagne, l'Espagne*). Most others are masculine (*le Canada, le Japon, le Portugal*). The big exception to remember is **le Mexique**, which ends in *-e* but is masculine.

## Why *Je viens de l'Australie* sounds wrong

Countries starting with a vowel use *d'* for both genders: *d'Australie* (feminine), *d'Iran* (masculine), *d'Argentine*. The article disappears, so you cannot tell the gender from the preposition. This is a relief.

## Sample responses

> **—** *Vous venez d'où ?*
>
> **—** *Je viens d'Australie, de Sydney.*

*D'où* means "from where." Another common question is *Vous êtes d'où ?*, answered with *Je suis de…*

## A mistake to avoid

In English, "I come from America" mentions the country as a bare name. In French, you cannot say *Je viens de États-Unis*. The plural requires *des États-Unis*, with *des* absorbing *de + les*. Remember the contraction rule from the pronunciation lessons: *de + les = des*.
