# Excuse Me vs Sorry vs Sorry-Sorry

English flattens a whole range of situations into one overworked word: "sorry." Sorry for bumping into you, sorry to interrupt, sorry I broke your vase, sorry for your loss — all the same word, doing a lot of very different jobs. French keeps these jobs separated, and using the right one is a small but genuine marker of fluency.

## The four-word toolkit

| Phrase | Literal meaning | What it's actually for |
|---|---|---|
| *Excusez-moi* / *Excuse-moi* | "Excuse me" | Getting attention, passing by someone, interrupting politely |
| *Pardon* | "Pardon" | Light physical bumps, minor "oops" moments, quick and reflexive |
| *Désolé(e)* | "Sorry" | A genuine apology for something you actually did or caused |
| *Je suis navré(e)* | "I am [deeply] sorry" | Formal, serious regret — bad news, real mistakes, higher-stakes apologies |

Read as a ladder, roughly by weight: *pardon* is the lightest, almost reflexive; *excusez-moi* is functional, about getting attention rather than apologizing at all; *désolé(e)* is a real apology; *je suis navré(e)* is a formal, heavier one. Reaching for the wrong end of that ladder — using *je suis navré(e)* for a light shoulder-bump, or a throwaway *pardon* after breaking something expensive — reads as mismatched in tone, the French equivalent of apologizing for spilling a drop of water as if you'd caused a flood.

## Excusez-moi: getting attention, not apologizing

This is the one that surprises English speakers most: *excusez-moi* is very often **not an apology at all** — it's a tool for claiming someone's attention before you've done anything wrong yet.

- Getting a waiter's attention: *"Excusez-moi !"*
- Asking a stranger for directions: *"Excusez-moi, où est la gare ?"*
- Squeezing past someone on a crowded train: *"Excusez-moi"*, said *before* you brush past them, not after

Used this way, it's functionally closer to "excuse me, but..." in English than to any apology — it's a doorway into a request, not regret for a mistake.

## Pardon: the reflexive one

*Pardon* is what comes out of your mouth automatically, with almost no thought, for the small physical collisions of daily life — stepping on someone's foot, a light shoulder bump in a crowd, cutting someone off by accident in a doorway. It's short, quick, low-stakes, and said in the moment, almost as a reflex rather than a considered apology.

> A useful mental model: *pardon* is what a French speaker says the way an English speaker might just wince and say "oop—" without fully forming a sentence.

## Désolé(e): the real apology

When you've actually done something — arrived late, forgotten something, made an error that inconvenienced someone — *désolé(e)* is the genuine, sincere "I'm sorry," carrying real weight rather than reflex:

> *"Désolé, je suis en retard !"* — "Sorry, I'm late!"
> *"Je suis désolée, j'ai oublié."* — "I'm sorry, I forgot."

Note the gendered ending again (*désolé* / *désolée*) — as with *enchanté(e)*, don't let it slow you down in conversation; it's barely audible in speech and not something a learner will be judged for getting "wrong" in the moment.

## Je suis navré(e): for when it actually matters

Reserve this one for genuinely serious situations — delivering bad news, a real professional error, formal written apologies. It would sound oddly heavy used for a minor bump on the street, the conversational equivalent of saying "I offer you my deepest condolences" after stepping on someone's shoe.

## Putting the ladder to use

> *[Bumping someone lightly in a crowd]* → "Pardon !"
> *[Trying to get a shopkeeper's attention]* → "Excusez-moi !"
> *[Arriving fifteen minutes late to meet a friend]* → "Désolé, je suis en retard !"
> *[A doctor delivering difficult news]* → "Je suis navré..."

Four different situations, four different words — and now, four correct ones instead of one overworked "sorry" trying to cover all of them at once.
