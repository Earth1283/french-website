# Tu vs Vous: The Decision You Make in 0.3 Seconds

Before you learn how to introduce yourself, you need the one piece of grammar that silently governs every single French conversation you'll ever have: the choice between *tu* and *vous*, both meaning "you."

English lost this distinction centuries ago — "you" is "you," whether you're addressing the Queen or your dog. French never lost it, and the choice you make the instant you open your mouth sets the entire tone, formality, and social distance of the conversation that follows.

## The two "you"s

- ***Tu*** — informal "you." Used with friends, family, children, peers your own age in casual settings, and (increasingly) coworkers at more relaxed companies.
- ***Vous*** — formal "you," **and also** the plural "you" (addressing more than one person, regardless of formality). Used with strangers, anyone older or in a position of authority, service staff, professional contacts, and essentially everyone by default until told otherwise.

This isn't just a word swap — it changes your verb conjugations too. *Tu as* vs *vous avez*. *Tu es* vs *vous êtes*. Getting the pronoun right and the verb wrong (or vice versa) is a very common beginner tell.

## The default assumption: start with *vous*

If you remember one rule from this page, make it this one: **with any adult stranger, default to *vous*.** Not because using *tu* is offensive — it's rarely a serious problem — but because *vous* is never wrong to start with, while *tu* to the wrong person can land as presumptuous or overly familiar, especially with someone older or in a professional context.

> Think of *vous* as a respectful, neutral starting distance. It costs you nothing to begin there, and moving *closer* (to *tu*) later is easy and welcome. Starting too close and having to back away is the awkward direction.

## Who gets automatic *tu*

Some relationships skip the *vous* phase entirely — no negotiation needed:

- **Children** — always *tu*, even from strangers
- **Animals** — also always *tu* (yes, this is a real rule people follow)
- **Your own family** — *tu*, universally
- **Close friends** — *tu*, once you're actually close
- Increasingly, **people your own age in casual, non-professional settings** — students, younger service staff, casual social meetups — though this varies by region and generation, with older speakers generally more conservative about it

## How the switch to *tu* actually happens

The move from *vous* to *tu* between two adults isn't automatic just because you've met a few times — it's often explicitly proposed, especially in slightly more formal contexts:

> *"On peut se tutoyer ?"* — "Can we use *tu* with each other?" (literally, "can we *tu*-each-other?")
> *"On peut se tutoyer, si tu veux !"* — the more casual, low-stakes version — "we can switch to *tu*, if you want!"

The verb here — ***tutoyer*** ("to address someone as *tu*") and its formal opposite ***vouvoyer*** ("to address someone as *vous*") — are real, commonly used verbs, and hearing them proposed to you is a genuinely nice social signal: it means the distance is closing.

## What actually happens if you get it wrong

Nobody will scold you. Getting *tu*/*vous* wrong as a visibly non-native speaker is one of the most forgiven mistakes in the entire language — French people extend a lot of grace here, because they know it's a genuinely hard concept for English speakers raised without it. The realistic outcomes, worst to mildest:

1. A very formal or older person you *tu*-ed too early might gently correct you, or simply seem a touch more reserved — rarely more than that
2. A younger, more relaxed person you *vous*-ed might laugh and say "oh, tu peux me tutoyer !" ("you can *tu* me!")
3. Most of the time: nothing happens at all, because context made your meaning perfectly clear anyway

The goal isn't perfection — it's building the habit of *noticing* the choice exists, and defaulting safe (*vous*) until you have a reason not to.
