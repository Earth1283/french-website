# The Bisou Minefield: A Practical Field Guide

The *bisou* — the cheek-kiss greeting — is the single most anxiety-inducing piece of French social choreography for visitors, mostly because nobody explains the actual rules before throwing you into a room where everyone's doing it. Here they are.

## What it actually is

A *bisou* is a light touch of cheeks (rarely an actual kiss on the skin, more of an air-kiss with a cheek-brush and a soft *mwah* sound) exchanged as a greeting or goodbye between people who know each other — friends, family, and often colleagues in more relaxed workplaces. It is **not** used with strangers, in most professional first-meetings, or in service interactions (nobody is *bisou*-ing their pharmacist).

## The number changes depending on where you are

This is the detail that catches even people who've lived in France a while: the number of kisses is **regional**, not universal, and there is no single "correct" number for the whole country.

| Region | Typical count |
|---|---|
| Paris and most of northern France | 2 (one per cheek) |
| Southern France (Provence, parts of the south) | Often 3 |
| Some western regions (parts of Brittany, Loire) | Sometimes 4 |
| Overall | 2 is the safest national default |

If you're unsure, 2 is the reasonable default almost everywhere, and if the other person goes for a third, you'll feel it and can simply follow — nobody minds a beat of uncertainty here, including French people themselves, who genuinely disagree with each other about this from region to region.

## Which side do you start on?

Also regional, also not something to stress over — but the general convention in most of France is to lean **right first** (your right cheek toward their right cheek), then switch. If you both guess differently, you'll gently bump noses for half a second, laugh, and correct — this happens to native speakers too, regularly. It is not a sign you've failed at French culture.

## When it applies — and when it very much doesn't

**Appropriate:**
- Greeting or leaving friends
- Family, of essentially any age gap
- Sometimes coworkers you know reasonably well, in relaxed workplace cultures, especially between women or in mixed-gender pairs

**Not appropriate:**
- First professional meetings — a **handshake** (*une poignée de main*) is the default here, full stop
- Service interactions — cashiers, waitstaff, pharmacists
- Strangers you're meeting for the first time socially — often a handshake first, with *bisous* only entering later once you actually know each other
- Any interaction where you're genuinely unsure — defaulting to a handshake is always safe; defaulting to a *bisou* with the wrong person is the actual minefield part

## What to do if you're caught off guard

If someone leans in for a *bisou* and you weren't expecting it: just go with it, lean slightly, let them lead the side and the count. If you go for a handshake and they go for a *bisou* (or the reverse), you'll get a brief, mutually amused half-second of miscoordination — genuinely common, genuinely not a big deal, and often actually a nice icebreaker rather than an embarrassment.

## Post-pandemic reality

Handshakes and *bisous* both took a real hit during COVID, and many people — especially in professional settings — have stayed slightly more reserved than pre-2020 norms. If in doubt in any ambiguous setting, a warm verbal greeting alone, with no physical contact offered, is now a completely normal and safe option. Follow the other person's lead rather than initiating.

## The one rule that actually matters

You will never get this fully "right" as a newcomer, because the rules genuinely vary by region, generation, and individual relationship — even French people negotiate it in real time constantly. What matters is: don't refuse an offered *bisou* awkwardly (that reads as cold), and don't force one on someone who's clearly extending a hand instead (that reads as presumptuous). Read the room, follow the lead, and treat the inevitable early mismatch as the charming, universal small failure that it is.
