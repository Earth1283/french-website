# Introducing Yourself Properly

Once *bonjour* and the *tu*/*vous* choice are handled, the next skill is the introduction itself — and French introductions follow a shape that's more scripted, and more forgiving, than you'd expect.

## The core exchange

The whole thing is really just two moves:

> *Person A:* "Comment vous appelez-vous ?" *(formal)* / "Comment tu t'appelles ?" *(informal)* — "What's your name?"
> *Person B:* "Je m'appelle [name]." — "My name is [name]."

Notice the verb: ***s'appeler*** — literally "to call oneself." You're not saying "I am called X" passively, you're saying "I call myself X," which is why the reflexive pronoun (*me/te/vous*) is doing real grammatical work here, not just decoration. This matters because you'll also use it to ask about someone *else's* name in the third person: *"Comment s'appelle-t-il ?"* — "What's his name?"

## Enchanté(e) — the payoff word

After the names are exchanged, the standard response is a single, satisfying word: ***enchanté***, or ***enchantée*** if you identify as feminine — literally "enchanted," functionally "pleased to meet you" or even just "nice to meet you."

It's worth noting this is genuinely one of the more elegant, low-effort-high-reward phrases in the whole language: one word, correctly deployed at the right beat in the conversation, and you sound considerably more polished than the sentence length would suggest. Don't overthink the gendered ending in the moment — in fast conversation, the *-e* is silent anyway, and getting it "wrong" is a non-issue for a learner.

## Asking where someone is from

Introductions rarely stop at names — origin is the natural next beat:

> *"Vous venez d'où ?"* / *"Tu viens d'où ?"* — "Where do you come from?"
> *"Je viens de..."* — "I come from..." *(a city)*
> *"Je viens des États-Unis / du Canada / d'Angleterre."* — "I come from the US / Canada / England."

That small shift — *de*, *des*, *du* — isn't random; it changes based on the gender and number of the country's name in French (*les* États-Unis is plural, so *des*; *le* Canada is masculine, so *du*). You'll meet this pattern properly in a later grammar-focused unit — for now, just recognize that the little word before the country isn't a typo.

## Introducing someone else

A genuinely useful, slightly more advanced move: bringing a third person into the conversation.

> *"Je vous présente [name]."* — "Let me introduce you to [name]" / "This is [name]." *(formal)*
> *"Je te présente [name]."* — same, informal.

This single phrase does a lot of social work at once — it signals politeness, includes everyone present, and gives the new person a natural moment to say their own *enchanté*.

## A complete, realistic mini-script

Putting the whole page together, here's what a first meeting actually sounds like, start to finish, at a reasonably formal register:

> **A:** Bonjour ! Comment vous appelez-vous ?
> **B:** Bonjour ! Je m'appelle Julien. Et vous ?
> **A:** Moi, c'est Claire. Enchantée.
> **B:** Enchanté. Vous venez d'où ?
> **A:** Je viens de Lyon. Et vous ?
> **B:** Moi, je viens du Canada.

Notice *"Moi, c'est Claire"* — a very natural, slightly more casual alternative to the full *"Je m'appelle Claire,"* the conversational equivalent of "me, I'm Claire." You'll hear this shortcut constantly once you're actually listening for it.
