# Common Mistakes & FAQ

A field guide to the specific ways learners get this wrong, and honest answers to the questions everyone actually has but rarely asks out loud.

## "Do I *really* have to say it to a self-checkout, or a cashier who's clearly slammed?"

Self-checkout: no, there's no person to greet — greet the humans, not the machines. A visibly slammed cashier at a manned register during a rush: a quick *bonjour* is still worth the half-second, but nobody expects the full ceremony (*bonjour, comment allez-vous* — save that for someone with actual time). Match your greeting's length to the moment. A rushed *"Bonjour, une baguette s'il vous plaît"* said as one breath is completely normal and, if anything, appreciated for its efficiency.

## "What if I forget and just start talking?"

It happens to everyone, including French people having an off day. It's genuinely not a catastrophe — it's a small faux pas, not an insult. The fix is easy and works retroactively: just say *bonjour* the moment you catch yourself, even mid-sentence, then continue. *"Oh — bonjour ! Est-ce que vous avez..."* People will barely register that you skipped a beat; they'll register that you caught it.

## "Is 'hello' ever OK, since so many French people speak English?"

In tourist-heavy spots in Paris, plenty of staff will happily switch to English the moment they hear an accent — and that's fine, follow their lead. But opening in English, rather than opening in French and *then* switching if needed, reads as assuming the room should accommodate you rather than the reverse. Lead with *bonjour* even if you know the conversation is about to become English; it costs one word and reliably changes the tone of everything that follows it, including how patient people are with the English part.

## "Is this a Paris thing, or everywhere in France?"

Everywhere, arguably *more* so outside Paris. Paris, being the country's most visited and most rushed city, is actually where the rule gets bent the most — a hurried Parisian might clip it to a fast *"'jour"* under their breath. In smaller towns and villages, the ritual is often slower, warmer, and more strictly observed; skipping it there stands out more, not less.

## "What about Quebec, or other French-speaking regions?"

This lesson focuses on France, where the *bonjour*-first rule is closest to a hard social norm. Québécois French shares the same core greeting but the retail-and-shopkeeper ritual around it is noticeably more relaxed — Quebec service culture leans closer to North American norms. Don't assume the etiquette transfers one-to-one between French-speaking regions; the vocabulary does, the social choreography doesn't always.

## "I said *bonjour* to someone and they didn't say it back — did I mess up?"

Almost certainly not you. Some people are just distracted, in a rush, or having a bad day, in France exactly like anywhere else. A skipped reply from one person isn't a verdict on your French or your manners — don't let one flat interaction talk you out of the habit. The rule holds on average, not in every single instance, and the average is what's worth building the reflex around.

## The one habit worth building

If you remember nothing else from this lesson: **before you speak to anyone in France, greet them first — even with just one word.** Everything else in this course gets easier once that reflex is automatic, because it buys you patience, warmth, and a little benefit of the doubt for every conjugation mistake you're about to make later.
