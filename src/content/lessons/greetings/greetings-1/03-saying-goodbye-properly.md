# Saying Goodbye Properly

Walking into a shop well and then mumbling your way out undoes half the good impression you just built. The French goodbye is a small ritual with its own shape, and once you know the pieces, it's genuinely satisfying to perform.

## The core word: *au revoir*

*Au revoir* — literally "until [we] see [each other] again" — is your reliable, all-purpose, always-safe goodbye. It works with strangers, shopkeepers, formal contacts, casual acquaintances, basically everyone except close friends in a hurry. When in doubt, this is the word.

## The full exit combo

A polished French goodbye rarely arrives alone. The standard, native-sounding pattern when leaving a shop, a service, or any brief interaction is a **two-part combo**:

> *"Au revoir, bonne journée !"* — "Goodbye, have a good day!"

Said as one breath, almost as a single unit. Dropping the second half doesn't make you rude, exactly — it just makes the exit feel clipped, unfinished, like leaving mid-sentence. Native speakers layer the well-wish on nearly every farewell, and it costs you two extra words for a noticeably warmer exit.

## The well-wish family — pick the one that fits

*Bonne journée* is only the most common member of a whole family of parting well-wishes. French has a specific one for almost every situation, and using the right one is a small but real marker of fluency:

| Phrase | Use it when |
|---|---|
| *Bonne journée* | General daytime goodbye — the default |
| *Bonne soirée* | Evening goodbye (see the previous page) |
| *Bonne fin de journée* | Late afternoon — "have a good rest of your day" |
| *Bon week-end* | Friday, or any goodbye heading into the weekend |
| *Bonne continuation* | To someone whose *activity* continues without you — e.g. leaving a queue, a class, a group tour |
| *Bon appétit* | Not a goodbye at all, but the reflexive thing said to anyone about to eat — including a stranger's table you walk past |
| *Bonne route* / *Bon voyage* | To someone about to drive or travel |

You will never be criticized for defaulting to *bonne journée* — but reaching for *bon week-end* on a Friday afternoon, correctly, is the kind of small detail that makes a French speaker's eyebrows go up in a good way.

## Casual goodbyes, for people you actually know

Among friends, the formal *au revoir* combo feels almost comically stiff — it's the equivalent of saying "farewell, and I bid you good day" to your roommate. Casual register has its own toolkit:

- ***Salut*** — doubles as hi *and* bye, entirely dependent on friends-only context (see the earlier lesson on *bonjour* vs *salut*)
- ***À plus*** (short for *à plus tard*) — "see you later," relaxed, extremely common among younger speakers
- ***À bientôt*** — "see you soon," works for both friends and slightly more polite goodbyes, sits in the middle of the register
- ***À demain*** — "see you tomorrow," used exactly when it's literally true
- ***À tout à l'heure*** — "see you in a bit," for someone you're about to see again *the same day*

## Putting it together

Here's a realistic full exchange, leaving a small shop in the early evening:

> *You:* "Merci, au revoir, bonne soirée !"
> *Shopkeeper:* "Merci à vous, bonne soirée !"

Two people, four total words each, and a complete, warm, correctly-timed goodbye. That's the whole skill — and it's now yours.
