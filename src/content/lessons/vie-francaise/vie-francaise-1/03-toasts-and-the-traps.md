# Toasts, Eye Contact, and the Traps of the Apéro

The toast is where most visitors reveal themselves. The words are short, but the gestures and the grammar matter. These are the mistakes English speakers make most often.

## *Santé* and *à votre santé*

*Santé* means "health," and it is the everyday toast among friends. *À votre santé* is the formal version, used with people you address as *vous*. In a group of strangers, or with an older guest, *à votre santé* signals good manners.

The noun is simple, but the grammar is easy to get wrong. *Santé* does not take an article when you use it as a toast. Saying *la santé* sounds like you are discussing health insurance.

## Eye contact is part of the toast

When you clink glasses, look each person in the eye. The superstition says that failing to do so brings bad luck, usually quoted as seven years. Whatever the origin, French people take the gesture seriously, and a glance at your glass instead of a face is noticed.

## The pastis trap

English speakers often add ice to every drink. Do not do this with a pastis. The French expect cold water added to the spirit, and ice first upsets the balance and the appearance. If you want it cold, ask for more water rather than ice.

## *Cul sec* is not a standard toast

*Cul sec* means "bottoms up," or "down in one." It is for close friends, and usually only when someone challenges you to finish a glass. Using it with a new group is a sign that you are trying too hard.

## The biggest trap: thinking *l'apéro* is happy hour

The French word has no discount, no bar counter, and no deadline. If you rush through your drink because you think the evening is ending, you have misunderstood the event. Let the first glass last, and let the conversation decide when it is time for dinner.
