# Ordering a Kir, a Pastis, and Something to Snack On

Ordering an aperitif is simple once you know the drinks and the grammar that lets you name them. The vocabulary is short, and the nouns follow predictable patterns.

## Two classic drinks

> *Un kir, s'il vous plaît.* — A kir, please.
>
> *Un pastis, s'il vous plaît.* — A pastis, please.

A *kir* is white wine with a splash of blackcurrant liqueur, *crème de cassis*. It is named after Canon Félix Kir, who popularised it in Burgundy after the Second World War. A *kir royal* is made with Champagne or sparkling wine instead of white wine. A *pastis* is an anise-flavoured spirit from the south of France, served with cold water. It turns cloudy when the water is added, and that cloudiness is normal.

Both nouns are masculine, so they take *un*. *Un kir* and *un pastis* are the same article pattern, which makes them easy to remember together.

## The indefinite article

*Un* is used for a masculine noun, and *une* for a feminine one. *Une bière* is feminine, while *un vin* is masculine. When you order several drinks, *deux* stays the same: *deux kirs, s'il vous plaît* means two kirs.

For snacks, the indefinite plural is *des*. *Des amuse-gueules* means "some small snacks," which is the phrase you want when you do not know the exact name of the item on the tray.

## Asking for water with a pastis

Pastis is traditionally served with a jug of water on the side. You can ask for it directly: *Vous avez de l'eau, s'il vous plaît ?* ("Do you have some water, please?"). Pour a measure of pastis, then add water slowly. The result is pleasantly cloudy, and nobody will think you have done it wrong.

## Snacks and the question of the bill

Snacks such as olives, crisps and small toasts keep the conversation going without replacing dinner. If the bowl is empty, ask for more: *Est-ce qu'on peut avoir encore des amuse-gueules ?* The question uses *on*, which is the natural way to ask the waiter for something for the table.
