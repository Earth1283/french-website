# What the Apéro Is For

The *apéro*, short for *apéritif*, is a drink before a meal, but it is also a social event with its own rules. If you misunderstand it, you may spend an evening wondering why nobody is eating. Understanding it is a much better plan.

## A ritual, not a drink

The *apéritif* is the transition between the working day and dinner. It usually happens somewhere between six and eight in the evening. Guests arrive, drinks are poured, and small snacks appear. Nobody rushes to the table, and that is the point. The conversation is the main course, and dinner comes later.

The word *apéro* is informal and very common. You will hear *prendre l'apéro* ("to have drinks before dinner") as easily as *prendre le café*. The phrase is a verb-object pair, so *prendre* does the work.

## The invitation

> *On prend l'apéro ?*

This four-word sentence is a genuine social invitation, not a request for a drink in a bar. *On* means "we" in everyday speech, and *prendre* is the verb you use for any drink or meal. Saying yes commits you to at least an hour of conversation, and most people treat that as the pleasant part.

Saying no is allowed, but a refusal is noticed. If you cannot come, offer a reason and an alternative: *Merci, mais je ne peux pas ce soir.* ("Thanks, but I can't tonight.")

## What it signals

The *apéro* tells you that the host considers you part of the household for an evening. Drinks come before the table, which makes the arrival relaxed. Offering to bring something small, such as bread, olives or a bottle of wine, is a polite gesture that French hosts appreciate, even if they say no.

## The cultural difference

English "happy hour" implies a bar, a discount, and a deadline. The *apéro* has none of these. It is slow, sociable, and usually free of obligation to leave early. If someone asks you to drink with them at six, relax and let the evening arrive at its own pace.
