# Traps English Speakers Fall Into When Lingering

Relaxed French hides a few traps. Most of them come from assuming that a café works like a fast-food counter, or that a stroll is a kind of task.

## Ordering once and assuming you must leave

Many visitors believe a single coffee is a transaction that ends when the cup is empty. In France, ordering one coffee and staying for an hour is entirely normal. Waiters will not hurry you, and they will not bring the bill unless you ask. If you want it, say *L'addition, s'il vous plaît.* Do not say *la facture*, which is the word for an invoice.

## *Pressé* is not *rapide*

If you want to say you are in a hurry, use *Je suis pressé(e)*. The English instinct is to say *Je suis rapide*, which means "I am fast," a description of speed rather than urgency. Learn *pressé* and you can refuse a rush politely.

## *Se promener* versus *se balader*

Both verbs mean to stroll, but *se balader* is informal. In an email to a professor you would write *se promener*. With friends, *on se balade ?* is the natural invitation. Choosing the wrong one does not cause confusion, but it does change the tone.

## *Perdu* is not always bad

English speakers often apologise for being lost. In French, *Je suis perdu(e)* can be a real emergency, but a relaxed wanderer might also say it happily. Context decides. If you are not in danger, you can simply enjoy not knowing where you are.

## *Terrasse* is not a patio

The word "terrace" in English can mean a flat roof or a garden. The French *terrasse* means the outdoor seating of a café. If you ask for a *terrasse* in a restaurant, you will be shown to a table outside, which is exactly what you want.

## The general rule

Lingering in France is a skill. Order slowly, stay as long as you like, and let the afternoon take its shape. The mistakes above are minor, but they all come from the same error: treating the café as a place to process customers rather than a place to be.
