# Futur Proche vs Futur Simple in Real Speech

French has two ways to talk about the future, and English speakers often reach for the wrong one out of habit. Both are correct French — the choice is about nuance, not correctness.

## Futur proche: *aller* + infinitive

Formed with the present tense of *aller* plus an infinitive, the futur proche is the workhorse of spoken French:

> *Je vais manger.* — I'm going to eat.
>
> *On va partir dans cinq minutes.* — We're leaving in five minutes.

It signals something close, decided, or already in motion — a plan you can practically see happening. Native speakers use it constantly in conversation, far more than the futur simple, for exactly this reason: most of what we say about the future in daily life is immediate and concrete.

## Futur simple: the one-word future

> *Je mangerai plus tard.* — I'll eat later (at some point, not right now).
>
> *Un jour, on partira vivre à la campagne.* — Someday we'll go live in the countryside.

The futur simple reaches further out, or covers things less certain, less immediate, or more formal. It's also the required form after *quand*, *dès que*, and similar time expressions when the event hasn't happened yet (chapter four), and it's what shows up in written predictions, contracts, and news reports.

## Choosing between them

A rough but reliable test: if you could point at a calendar or a clock and say "that's when," futur proche usually fits better. If the timing is vague, distant, or you're speaking formally, reach for futur simple.

> *Je vais appeler le médecin* (right now, I've decided) vs. *Je l'appellerai un jour* (eventually, no rush).

In spoken French, futur proche also often replaces futur simple even where futur simple would technically work, simply because it feels more natural in conversation. The reverse is rarer: you won't usually hear futur proche in a weather forecast or a legal document — those default to futur simple.

## A register note

Futur proche skews informal and oral. Futur simple skews written, formal, and literary, though it's completely normal in speech too — it doesn't sound stiff the way, say, an overly formal vocabulary choice might. Think of the difference less as "casual vs. correct" and more as "immediate vs. everything else." Both belong in your everyday French; you just need to feel which one fits the moment.

## One trap to avoid

Don't build a futur proche out of *aller* + an infinitive of *aller* itself to mean "going to go" — French simply says *je vais aller* is technically fine but sounds clunky; natives usually just say *j'irai* (futur simple) or *je pars* (present) instead. When in doubt with *aller*, the futur simple *j'irai* is usually the more natural pick.
