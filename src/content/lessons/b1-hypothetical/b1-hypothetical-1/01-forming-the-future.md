# Building the Futur Simple

The futur simple is French's plain, no-frills future: *I will speak, you will arrive, we will finish*. Unlike English, which glues "will" in front of the verb, French bolts a fixed set of endings onto a stem, and the whole thing becomes one word.

## The six endings

Every verb in the futur simple — regular or not — uses the same six endings:

- *-ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont*

These happen to look almost exactly like the present tense of *avoir* (ai, as, a, avons, avez, ont), which is not a coincidence: historically the futur simple grew out of the infinitive plus *avoir*. That history is a decent memory hook, but treat the endings as fixed and move on.

## Regular verbs: stem is the infinitive

For regular *-er* and *-ir* verbs, the stem is simply the infinitive:

> *parler* → *je parlerai, tu parleras, il parlera...*
>
> *finir* → *je finirai, tu finiras, elle finira...*

For *-re* verbs, drop the final *-e* before adding the endings:

> *vendre* → *je vendrai, tu vendras, nous vendrons...*
>
> *prendre* → *je prendrai, vous prendrez, ils prendront...*

## Spelling quirks to watch for

A handful of *-er* verbs shift their spelling in the futur simple because the stem vowel is unstressed and French spelling tracks pronunciation:

- Verbs like *acheter* and *lever* add a grave accent throughout: *j'achèterai*, *je lèverai*.
- Verbs like *appeler* and *jeter* double the consonant instead: *j'appellerai*, *je jetterai*.
- Verbs ending in *-yer* (*payer*, *nettoyer*, *employer*) change *y* to *i*: *je paierai*, *je nettoierai*. This one is mandatory for *-oyer* and *-uyer* verbs, optional (but common) for *-ayer* verbs like *payer*, which can also stay *je payerai*.

None of this touches the endings — only the stem's spelling wobbles.

## What the futur simple is actually for

In real French, the futur simple covers:

- Predictions and plans further out than "later today": *Il pleuvra ce week-end* (It'll rain this weekend).
- Promises and formal commitments: *Je vous enverrai le contrat demain* (I'll send you the contract tomorrow).
- Instructions dressed up as statements, especially in writing: *Vous remplirez ce formulaire* (You'll fill out this form — functionally a polite command).
- Anything after *quand*, *dès que*, *aussitôt que* when the event is still ahead — more on that trap in chapter four.

Compare that to the near future (*futur proche*, chapter three), which leans toward things already decided or about to happen. The futur simple, by contrast, can describe events that are vague, distant, or purely hypothetical — closer to English "will" than to "going to."

One last thing before you memorize ten irregular stems: the futur simple is a single word. There's no auxiliary to conjugate separately, no agreement to worry about, no *avoir* or *être* to choose. Once you have the stem, the rest is copy-paste. That's the good news. The stems, unfortunately, are where French keeps its surprises — which is exactly where chapter two picks up.
