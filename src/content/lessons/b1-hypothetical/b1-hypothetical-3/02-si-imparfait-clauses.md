# Si + Imparfait: The Trap That Catches Everyone

This is the chapter to read twice. The rule itself is short; the mistake it prevents is one of the most common errors English speakers make in French, at any level.

## The pattern

**Si + imparfait → conditionnel présent.** This combination describes something hypothetical, unlikely, or contrary to present reality — a "what if" rather than a realistic plan.

> *Si j'avais de l'argent, je voyagerais.*
> — If I had money, I would travel. (implying: I don't, currently)
>
> *Si elle parlait plus lentement, je la comprendrais.*
> — If she spoke more slowly, I would understand her.
>
> *Si nous habitions à Paris, nous irions au musée chaque semaine.*
> — If we lived in Paris, we'd go to the museum every week.

In every example, the *si*-clause imagines a different reality from the one that currently holds, and the result clause spells out what would follow from it.

## The trap: never conditionnel after si

English speakers, hearing "would" in the result clause, often reach for a conditionnel in the *if*-clause too:

> ❌ *Si j'aurais de l'argent, je voyagerais.*
> ✅ *Si j'avais de l'argent, je voyagerais.*

This is wrong every time, with no exceptions, no matter which meaning of *si* is in play ("if," "whether," or otherwise introducing a hypothesis). French reserves the conditionnel exclusively for the result clause. The *si*-clause only ever takes présent, imparfait, or (for a third, more advanced pattern involving the past) plus-que-parfait — never conditionnel, ever.

If you remember nothing else from this unit, remember this: **si + conditionnel is always wrong.** It's worth saying the correct pair out loud enough times that it becomes reflexive: *si j'avais... je voyagerais*, not *si j'aurais*.

## Why the imparfait, specifically

The imparfait here isn't marking the past — it's marking distance from reality, the same distancing job the conditionnel does in the result clause. *Si j'avais de l'argent* doesn't mean "when I used to have money"; it means "in a version of reality where I have money, which isn't this one." French recycles the imparfait's form for this purely because it already existed and sounded right — not because the sentence is about the past.

## Comparing the two si-patterns side by side

> *Si tu viens, je serai content.* — realistic: you might well come.
>
> *Si tu venais, je serais content.* — hypothetical: you probably won't, but imagine if.

Same basic idea, two different degrees of likelihood, signaled entirely by the tense pair chosen — présent/futur for the realistic version, imparfait/conditionnel for the hypothetical one. Swapping *venais* for *viendrais*, or *serai* for *serais*, would break the pairing and sound foreign to a native ear.

## Recap

- Si + imparfait + conditionnel présent = hypothetical, "what if."
- Never conditionnel directly after *si* — this is the single most important rule in this unit.
- The imparfait signals distance from reality, not the past.
