# Giving Advice and Reporting Rumors with the Conditionnel

Beyond politeness, the conditionnel présent has two more everyday jobs: telling someone what they should do, and passing along information you can't fully vouch for. Both rely on the same distancing effect — "this isn't a fact, it's a possibility."

## Advice: *devrais* and *à ta place*

The conditionnel of *devoir* is the standard way to say "should":

> *Tu devrais dormir plus.* — You should sleep more.
>
> *Vous devriez consulter un médecin.* — You should see a doctor.

A more vivid way to give advice frames it as if you were in the other person's shoes:

> *À ta place, je démissionnerais.* — In your place, I'd quit.
>
> *Si j'étais toi, j'accepterais l'offre.* — If I were you, I'd accept the offer.

Both patterns put the actual suggestion in the conditionnel (*démissionnerais, accepterais*) — the "if" part can be a full clause with *si*, or just a short frame like *à ta place*. Either way, no direct order is given; the conditionnel keeps it feeling like a suggestion, not a command.

## Impersonal judgments: *il serait*

> *Il serait utile de réserver à l'avance.* — It would be useful to book in advance.
>
> *Il serait dommage de rater ça.* — It would be a shame to miss that.

This impersonal *il serait* pattern is common in written advice, guides, and reviews — a gentler alternative to *il faut* (you must), which can sound more forceful.

## Reporting unconfirmed news

French journalism and everyday gossip both use the conditionnel to flag information that hasn't been officially confirmed:

> *Le président serait en visite la semaine prochaine.*
> — The president is reportedly visiting next week. (not officially confirmed)
>
> *Selon des témoins, l'accident aurait fait plusieurs blessés.*
> — According to witnesses, the accident reportedly injured several people.

Compare the same fact stated as confirmed, in the regular tenses:

> *Le président est en visite.* — The president is visiting. (fact)
>
> *Le président sera en visite.* — The president will be visiting. (a scheduled, confirmed fact)

Swapping in the conditionnel — *serait* instead of *est* or *sera* — is the entire signal. There's no extra word for "allegedly" or "reportedly"; the verb form does that work by itself. Watch for this in French news headlines: a conditionnel verb is French's built-in disclaimer.

## Holding it together

Across all three uses — politeness, advice, unconfirmed news — the conditionnel's job stays consistent: it marks distance from plain fact. A request softened, a suggestion offered rather than ordered, a rumor flagged as unverified. The next chapter puts all of it to work in a full practice set.
