# Traps English Speakers Fall Into in the Room

English gives you a clear word for every complaint about a room. French often gives you a phrase that looks like a translation and means something else.

## *J'ai chaud* is “I'm hot”

English speakers say “I'm hot” and reach for *Je suis chaud*, which does not describe your body temperature. For temperature, use *avoir chaud* or *avoir froid*: *J'ai chaud*, “I'm hot,” and *J'ai froid*, “I'm cold.” The room is hot in French as *Il fait chaud*, using the impersonal *il*. If the room is the problem, say *Il fait trop chaud dans la chambre*.

## *Vue sur* versus *vue de*

Covered in the last chapter, but worth repeating: *une vue sur la mer*, not *une vue de la mer*. The preposition is the trap, not the noun.

## *Le parking* is the car park

English speakers may say “parking lot” or “parking garage.” In French, *le parking* covers the whole thing. *Un parking* is the place, *le parking* is the general concept. The hotel will understand you if you ask *Est-ce que l'hôtel a un parking ?*

## *Un ascenseur* is not always spacious

*L'ascenseur* is the lift. In older Parisian buildings it may be the size of a telephone box. The trap is the word *lift*: French never says *le lift* for this, and the word *ascenseur* is the one that hotel staff recognize.

## *Changer*, *échanger*, *rendre*

Do not use *échanger* to mean “change rooms.” *Échanger* means to exchange, for example a gift or a phone. *Changer de chambre* is the right phrase. The word you want for returning a towel or a faulty item is *rendre*: *Je peux vous rendre cette serviette ?*

## The fallback when staff are unsure

If someone says *Je ne sais pas* (“I don't know”), ask *Qui peut m'aider ?* (“Who can help me?”). Then ask again, slowly, with the nouns you already know. You are not failing to speak French. You are finding the person who can say yes.
