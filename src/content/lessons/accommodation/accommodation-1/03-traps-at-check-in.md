# Traps English Speakers Fall Into at Check-in

English gives you direct equivalents for most hotel words. French often gives you a different image, and that is where the errors happen.

## *Je suis réservé* is not your booking

English speakers sometimes reach for *Je suis réservé* to mean “I have a reservation.” The sentence means “I am reserved,” as in shy or taciturn. A receptionist will be amused, politely, and will still not find your name faster. Use *J'ai une réservation*.

## *Le déjeuner* is lunch

As covered in the first chapter, *le petit-déjeuner* is breakfast. Dropping the *petit-* turns your question into a lunch question. A surprisingly common mistake.

## *Une clé* versus *une carte*

English says “key card” as a single item. French hotels may mean either a metal *clé* or a plastic *carte magnétique*. If you ask *Où est ma clé ?* when you have been given a card, the staff will politely point out the card in your hand. Ask *Est-ce que c'est une carte ou une clé ?* if you genuinely cannot tell.

## *Le check-in* is understood, but...

The anglicism *le check-in* is widely used in France, especially in hotels and airports. It works. But if the receptionist does not react, switch to *l'enregistrement*, the French word for registration, which is the more formal term at a desk.

## Do not translate “it doesn't work” too directly

*Ça ne marche pas* is common and perfectly acceptable in conversation, but *ne fonctionne pas* is the phrase you want for a device or system. It sounds more precise and avoids the impression that your room is being unreasonable.

## Watch the accent

*À quelle heure* needs the accent on *à*. Without it, *a* is the verb *avoir* in the third person and the sentence collapses. A small accent, a large difference.
