# Arriving at the Front Desk Without Panic

The check-in desk decides how the rest of your stay will go. The receptionist is juggling a queue, a phone and a screen, and your first three sentences tell them whether you are an expected guest or a problem. Learn a short sequence and you will be handled as the former.

## Open with your reservation

Start with the reason you are there:

> *J'ai une réservation.* — I have a reservation.
>
> *Au nom de Chen.* — Under the name Chen.

*Une réservation* is feminine, which is why it takes *une*. *Au nom de* means “in the name of” and is the standard way to say which name the booking is under. If the receptionist asks *C'est à quel nom ?* (“Under which name is it?”), this is the answer you give. Spell an unusual surname slowly, letter by letter.

## Ask for what you need

Once the booking is found, you will usually need your key and a few basics:

> *Ma clé, s'il vous plaît.* — My key, please.
>
> *Le WiFi ne fonctionne pas.* — The WiFi isn't working.
>
> *Il n'y a pas d'eau chaude.* — There's no hot water.

If a porter offers to carry your bags, *Ma chambre, s'il vous plaît* (“My room, please”) tells them where you are going and saves the awkward pause. *Il n'y a pas* is the negative of *il y a* (“there is”). The *de* becomes *d'* before a vowel, so *d'eau*. The *il* is an impersonal subject, the same one you hear in *il fait chaud* (“it's hot”). You do not need to know the grammar to use the sentence, but it explains why the sentence never needs a real person as its subject.

## Breakfast, not lunch

Ask about breakfast with *À quelle heure est le petit-déjeuner ?* Note the word *déjeuner* on its own means lunch. A French friend who tells you to meet at *le déjeuner* is not suggesting breakfast. Dinner is *le dîner*. The hotel will understand you either way, but your breakfast plans will not survive the confusion.

## Keys and cards

Modern hotels often give you a *carte magnétique*, a plastic key card. Older hotels still use a *clé*, a metal key, and occasionally a key attached to a large wooden fob. Both are feminine. If the staff hand you a card and say *Voici votre carte*, your room number is on the reception slip or the sleeve.

Keep the sentences short. A calm, plain request beats a long apology every time.
