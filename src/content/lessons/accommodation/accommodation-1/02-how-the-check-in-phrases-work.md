# How the Check-in Sentences Are Built

Most check-in French is built from a small set of patterns. Once you see them, you can swap in new nouns without memorizing every sentence.

## *J'ai* plus a noun

*J'ai une réservation* uses *avoir*, “to have,” in the present tense. Its forms are *j'ai*, *tu as*, *il a*, *nous avons*, *vous avez*, *ils ont*. Hotels generally need the formal *vous* form, which is why you may hear *Vous avez une réservation ?* from the desk, and you can reply with *Oui, j'ai une réservation*. The pattern works for almost any need: *J'ai besoin d'une clé* (“I need a key”).

## The *au nom de* pattern

*Au nom de* is a fixed phrase followed by a name. *Au nom de Dubois. Au nom de Martin.* The contraction *au* comes from *à + le*, the same contraction you meet in *au restaurant*. Keep the phrase whole and you do not need to think about it.

## Negation with *ne... pas*

Look at *Le WiFi ne fonctionne pas.* The negation wraps around the verb: *ne* goes before it, *pas* after it. In relaxed speech, *ne* often disappears and you hear *le wifi fonctionne pas*. Standard listeners still understand it, and you should recognize it when you hear it. For your own sentences, keep *ne* and *pas*.

## The *il* that means “there”

*Il y a* means “there is” or “there are,” and it is invariable. *Il y a une erreur*, *Il y a deux clés*. The negative is *il n'y a pas de* or *il n'y a pas d'*, depending on whether the noun starts with a vowel or consonant sound. *Il n'y a pas de savon* (“there's no soap”) and *il n'y a pas d'eau chaude* (“there's no hot water”).

## Articles and gender

*La clé*, *la carte*, *une réservation*, *l'eau*. Gender decides the article, and *une* or *un* marks a feminine or masculine noun. When you learn a new word, learn it with its article. Future you will be grateful.
