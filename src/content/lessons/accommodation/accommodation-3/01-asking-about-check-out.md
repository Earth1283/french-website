# Asking About Check-out Without Losing Your Morning

Check-out is the last chance to fix problems before they become your problem at home. You want three answers: when you must leave, whether you can leave your bags, and what you owe. French gives you a clear way to ask each one.

## What time do I have to leave?

> *À quelle heure dois-je libérer la chambre ?* — What time do I need to vacate the room?

*Libérer* means to free up or vacate, and it is the everyday hotel verb. *Dois-je* is the inverted form of *je dois*, “I must.” Inversion is formal and polite, and it is exactly what the desk expects. If you want a simpler version, say *Je dois partir à quelle heure ?* in a more casual tone.

Standard French hotels set check-out around midday. If your flight leaves in the evening, ask about a later departure rather than hoping for the best.

## Telling them when you leave

> *Je voudrais partir demain matin.* — I'd like to leave tomorrow morning.

*Partir* means to leave. It is irregular in the present and past, but for check-out you need only the infinitive after *je voudrais*. Time expressions go at the end: *demain matin* (tomorrow morning), *ce soir* (this evening), *lundi* (Monday).

## Asking to leave your bags

> *Puis-je laisser mes bagages ici ?* — Can I leave my bags here?

*Puis-je* is the inverted form of *je peux*, “I can,” and it is the polite way to ask for permission. *Laisser* means to leave something behind, and *mes bagages* means your luggage. This is the phrase for when your flight is late and your room is not.

## Calling a taxi

> *Pouvez-vous appeler un taxi ?* — Could you call a taxi?

The pattern is the same *Pouvez-vous* plus infinitive from the room chapter. The word *appeler* means “to call,” so the sentence literally asks the hotel to call one on your behalf.

## Practice tip

Say all four questions aloud, with the inversion intact, before you reach the desk. The inversion is the one thing that feels unnatural, and it is the part that makes the request sound right.
