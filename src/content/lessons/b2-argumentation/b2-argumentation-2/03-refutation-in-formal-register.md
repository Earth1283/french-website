# Refutation in Formal Register

Once you've conceded a point with *certes* or *bien que*, you need vocabulary to deliver the refutation itself — and to do it in a register that matches the rest of a formal argument, rather than dropping into casual *mais* every time.

## Il n'en reste pas moins que

Literally "it remains no less true that," this phrase acknowledges a preceding point while insisting your own still stands. It's stronger and more formal than a plain "but," and it works well right after a *certes* clause.

> *Certes, la réforme a des défauts. Il n'en reste pas moins qu'elle a permis de créer des emplois.* — Granted, the reform has flaws. The fact remains that it created jobs.

## Force est de constater que

This fixed phrase — "one is forced to observe that" — announces an unwelcome or inconvenient conclusion, as though the facts themselves left you no choice. It's common in journalism and formal essays, and it carries real rhetorical weight precisely because it sounds reluctant rather than eager.

> *Force est de constater que le système a échoué.* — One must admit that the system has failed.

Note the fixed word order: *force est de* + infinitive, never *il est force de* or similar variations.

## Toutefois and néanmoins

Both mean "however" or "nevertheless" and sit a register above plain *mais*. They are close to interchangeable in essays; native writers often alternate between the two simply to avoid repeating the same word.

> *L'idée est séduisante ; toutefois, elle reste difficile à financer.* — The idea is appealing; however, it remains hard to fund.
>
> *L'argument est solide ; néanmoins, il reste contestable.* — The argument is solid; nevertheless, it remains debatable.

## Contrairement à ce que l'on pourrait penser

A longer, more literary opener for refuting a common assumption head-on, rather than a specific preceding sentence. It works well at the start of a paragraph that's about to overturn received wisdom.

> *Contrairement à ce que l'on pourrait penser, cette loi profite aux petites entreprises.* — Contrary to what one might think, this law benefits small businesses.

## Choosing between them

- *Il n'en reste pas moins que* — directly answers a concession you just made.
- *Force est de constater que* — announces a conclusion as unavoidable.
- *Toutefois / néanmoins* — a general-purpose formal "however," interchangeable with each other.
- *Contrairement à ce que l'on pourrait penser* — opens by overturning an assumption, not a specific sentence.

Reach for the one that matches what you're actually doing, not just the closest English word — that's what separates a fluent-sounding refutation from a translated one.
