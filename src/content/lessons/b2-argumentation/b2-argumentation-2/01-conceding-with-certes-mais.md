# Conceding With Certes... Mais

If the last lesson gave you the skeleton of an argument, this one gives you its most French move: conceding a point on purpose, so you can dismantle it a sentence later. English speakers tend to avoid conceding anything — it feels like losing. French argumentation treats it as the opposite: refusing to concede a fair point makes you look like you haven't actually thought about the question.

## The pattern

*Certes* opens the concession; *mais* delivers the real argument. Together they form a single rhetorical unit, almost always in that order.

> *Certes, le train est cher, mais il est rapide.* — Granted, the train is expensive, but it's fast.
>
> *Certes, cette mesure est impopulaire, mais elle est nécessaire.* — Granted, this measure is unpopular, but it's necessary.

The concession must be genuine — a real point the other side would make, not a weak version of it. A strawman concession ("granted, some people might possibly disagree slightly") fools no one and undercuts your credibility. Give the opposing point its full weight, then show why it doesn't settle the question.

## Why concede at all?

Three reasons this works better than ignoring the counterpoint:

- It proves you understand the issue well enough to state the other side accurately.
- It disarms the reader's own objection before they can raise it — there's nothing left to interrupt with.
- It sets up a much stronger "mais," because the reader has just watched you take the opposing view seriously.

> *Certes, on ne peut pas résoudre le chômage du jour au lendemain, mais des mesures ciblées peuvent en réduire l'ampleur.* — Granted, unemployment can't be solved overnight, but targeted measures can reduce its scale.

## A word on tone

*Certes* by itself, without a following *mais*, can also stand alone in speech as a slightly ironic "sure, sure" — acknowledging a point while visibly unconvinced. In formal writing, though, always pair it with *mais* (or a synonym like *cependant* or *toutefois*, covered later in this lesson) — a dangling *certes* with no rebuttal reads as unfinished.

- *Certes* concedes; *mais* rebuts. They travel together in writing.
- Make the concession real and specific, not a token gesture.
- A strong *certes... mais* is often more persuasive than ignoring the counterpoint entirely.
