# Connectors That Build an Argument

English essays often just start a new sentence and trust the reader to follow. French argumentative writing refuses to trust anyone that much. Every step of the reasoning gets a signpost, and the choice of signpost tells the reader exactly what kind of step is coming: a new point, an addition to the same point, or a shift to a different one. Learning these connectors is not decoration — it is the skeleton the whole argument hangs on.

## Opening the list

Start a sequence of points with *tout d'abord* in speech and in most writing, or *en premier lieu* when you want a noticeably more formal register — a cover letter, a dissertation, a work email to your boss's boss.

> *Tout d'abord, il faut définir les termes du débat.* — First of all, we need to define the terms of the debate.
>
> *En premier lieu, examinons les chiffres.* — In the first place, let's examine the figures.

Both mean "first," but *en premier lieu* signals that a numbered, structured argument is coming — it rarely appears alone, the way *tout d'abord* sometimes does in casual speech.

## Stacking versus pivoting

This is where English speakers get sloppy, because English "furthermore," "moreover," and "besides" all blur into each other. French keeps them apart:

- *en outre* and *de surcroît* pile another reason onto the SAME point you were just making. *De surcroît* is the more literary of the two — save it for writing, not conversation.
- *par ailleurs* pivots to a related but distinct point, closer to English "incidentally" or "on another note."

> *Cette mesure est coûteuse. En outre, elle est inefficace.* — This measure is costly. Furthermore, it's inefficient. (same point, reinforced)
>
> *Cette mesure est coûteuse. Par ailleurs, elle a été mal expliquée au public.* — This measure is costly. Besides, it was poorly explained to the public. (a separate point)

Native writers notice when you swap these. Getting it right is a fast way to sound like you actually studied French argumentation, not just French vocabulary.

## The traps

Two habits sink English speakers here. First, defaulting to *et* or *aussi* for every kind of addition — flat and repetitive, where French essays want variety and precision. Second, treating *par ailleurs* as a synonym for *en outre* because both translate loosely as "moreover" in a dictionary — they are not interchangeable to a French reader, who expects the pivot.

- Use *tout d'abord* / *en premier lieu* to open.
- Use *en outre* / *de surcroît* to reinforce the same point.
- Use *par ailleurs* to move to a related but separate point.
- Save *de surcroît* for writing — it sounds stiff out loud.

Master this handful and your paragraphs stop reading like a list and start reading like an argument.
