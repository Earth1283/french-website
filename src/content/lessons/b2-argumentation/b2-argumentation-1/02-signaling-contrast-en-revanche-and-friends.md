# Signalling Contrast Without Sounding Blunt

Every argument eventually needs to turn a corner — to introduce an opposing view, a counterweight, a "but." English has one workhorse word for this: "but." French essay writing treats that as a blunt instrument and reaches for something more controlled.

## En revanche: the essay's favourite pivot

*En revanche* means "on the other hand" or "conversely," and it is the safest, most versatile contrast connector in formal French. It is sharper than a limp *mais*, and — importantly — more acceptable in careful writing than *par contre*, which many teachers and editors still consider slightly too casual for a dissertation, even though it is extremely common in speech.

> *Le projet est coûteux. En revanche, il créerait des centaines d'emplois.* — The project is costly. On the other hand, it would create hundreds of jobs.
>
> *En revanche, les partisans du projet soulignent ses bénéfices écologiques.* — On the other hand, supporters of the project highlight its ecological benefits.

Use *en revanche* when the second idea genuinely counterbalances the first — not just any change of topic.

## D'une part... d'autre part: holding both sides at once

While *en revanche* contrasts two separate sentences, *d'une part... d'autre part* frames both sides of an issue within the same paragraph, almost the same breath. It's the structural move for presenting a balanced, two-sided picture before you take a stance.

> *D'une part, le télétravail réduit la fatigue ; d'autre part, il isole certains salariés.* — On the one hand, remote work reduces fatigue; on the other, it isolates some employees.

Note the punctuation: a semicolon or a period typically separates the two halves, and both halves should be grammatically parallel — same kind of clause on each side. Lopsided pairs (a full clause on one side, a fragment on the other) read as sloppy in French the same way they do in English.

## Why this matters beyond grammar

Underneath the vocabulary is a cultural expectation: a French argumentative text is supposed to show that you have weighed both sides before judging either. Jumping straight to your opinion, English-essay-style, can come across as underdeveloped. Structuring a genuine back-and-forth — *d'une part... d'autre part*, then *en revanche* to sharpen the tension — is what makes an argument read as considered rather than reactive.

- *En revanche* — pivots between two ideas across sentences.
- *D'une part... d'autre part* — lays out two sides within one move.
- Keep both halves of *d'une part... d'autre part* grammatically parallel.
- Avoid *par contre* in formal writing, even though it's everywhere in speech.

Get comfortable with both, and you can build a paragraph that argues fairly before it argues hard.
