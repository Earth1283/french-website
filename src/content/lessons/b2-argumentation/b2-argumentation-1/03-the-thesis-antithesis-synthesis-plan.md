# The Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis Plan

Ask a French adult how they learned to argue, and many will mention the same three words: *thèse*, *antithèse*, *synthèse*. This is the essay plan hammered into students from collège through the baccalauréat, and it explains a lot about how French people structure spoken debate too — including the connectors from the last two chapters.

## The three movements

- **La thèse**: you develop one position on the question, with its strongest supporting points, as if it were the only reasonable view.
- **L'antithèse**: you then develop the opposing position, just as seriously and just as fairly — not as a weak strawman to knock down, but as a real, defensible view.
- **La synthèse**: you close by reconciling the two, often showing that the truth depends on context, or proposing a nuanced third position that keeps what's valid in both.

> *Le plan thèse-antithèse-synthèse structure la dissertation française depuis des générations.* — The thesis-antithesis-synthesis plan has structured the French dissertation for generations.

This is very different from the standard English five-paragraph essay, which usually states a thesis in the introduction and spends the whole essay defending it. A French examiner reading an essay that only ever defends one side may mark it down as unbalanced, however well-argued — the form itself is expected to show two-sidedness.

## Where the connectors slot in

Everything from the previous two chapters exists to build this shape:

- *Tout d'abord* / *en premier lieu* opens the thesis section.
- *En outre* / *de surcroît* reinforces points within it.
- *En revanche* — or a full paragraph break — marks the turn into the antithesis.
- *D'une part... d'autre part* can appear inside either section, or right at the pivot, to hold both views up for a moment before choosing one to develop.
- *En conclusion* / *pour conclure* opens the synthesis.

> *En conclusion, ce débat reste ouvert, mais les deux positions apportent des éléments valables.* — In conclusion, this debate remains open, but both positions bring valid points to the table.

*Pour conclure* is very slightly less stiff than *en conclusion* — both are entirely correct, and the choice is closer to style than meaning.

## Why it's worth learning even if you'll never write a dissertation

This plan isn't just an exam format — it's a habit of mind. French public debate, op-eds, and even casual arguments often move through this same rhythm: concede the other side has a point, then argue anyway. Recognising the shape helps you follow spoken French debate in real time, and reproducing it — even loosely — makes your own arguments sound native rather than translated.

- Thesis, then antithesis, then synthesis — not thesis defended from start to finish.
- Give the opposing side a genuinely strong version, not a strawman.
- The synthesis should feel earned, not just "both sides have a point."
