# Where the Essentials Go Wrong

Slang is easy to pick up and easy to misuse. The problem is rarely the vocabulary. It is the register: how formal the situation is, and who is listening.

## *Nul* is for things, not people

*C'est nul* about a film is harmless. Calling a person *nul* is an insult. Saying *Il est nul* about a colleague is blunt enough to end a friendship, so keep the word attached to objects and plans.

## *Laisse tomber* is for friends

*Laisse tomber* uses the *tu* form. Saying it to a shopkeeper, a landlord or an older stranger sounds rude. With people you address as *vous*, switch to *Oubliez ça* or *Ce n'est pas grave*, which means "it's not serious."

## *Sympa* is not "sympathetic"

English "sympathetic" means showing compassion. The French *sympathique* means "nice" or "pleasant," and *sympa* is the casual version. Someone who feels sorry for you is *compatissant*, not *sympa*.

## *Zut* in writing

*Zut* is fine in a message to a friend. In a formal email or a letter of complaint, it is too casual. The exclamation belongs to speech.

## Translating "Darn!" literally

Some learners reach for *Quel dommage !* for every mild disappointment. That phrase means "What a shame!" and is better for consoling someone than for reacting to your own mishap. For a dropped ice cream, *Zut !* or *Oh là là !* is more natural.

## Overloading one word

A single slang word repeated in every sentence sounds like a tic, not fluency. If *sympa* appears three times in one conversation, the effect is closer to a parrot than a friend. Mix in standard alternatives such as *c'est bien*, *c'est agréable* or *j'aime bien*, and keep the slang for the moments that call for it.

## Slang in writing with strangers

A text to a classmate can say *c'est nul*. A message to a landlord should not. When you write, check whether you would say the same words aloud to the person in front of you. If not, use the standard form.
