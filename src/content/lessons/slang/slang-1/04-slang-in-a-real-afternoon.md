# Slang in a Real Afternoon

Here is a casual exchange with a friend, Léa, at a café. Notice that everyone uses *tu*, and that the slang appears where a native speaker would use it.

> **Léa:** *Tu es en retard. Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé ?*
>
> **You:** *Le métro, c'est la galère. Laisse tomber.*
>
> **Léa:** *D'accord. Tu as vu le nouveau film ?*
>
> **You:** *Oui. Honnêtement, c'est nul.*
>
> **Léa:** *Sérieux ? Moi, je trouve ça sympa.*
>
> **You:** *Zut ! Ma glace est tombée.*
>
> **Léa:** *Tu as l'air fatigué, tu sais.*

The first reply uses *la galère* as a noun, which is why it follows *c'est* with the article. *Laisse tomber* closes a topic you would rather not expand. *Moi, je trouve ça sympa* is a common spoken construction that puts emphasis on the speaker's opinion.

## When it goes wrong

Sometimes you will not recognise a word. Say *Pardon, ça veut dire quoi, « galère » ?* — Sorry, what does "galère" mean? Friends are usually happy to explain, and they enjoy hearing a learner ask. If the word is used in a formal setting, simply ask *Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?* and switch to standard French.

The safest rule is to listen first, copy a word once you understand it, and use it only when the setting matches.

## What to take from the exchange

Notice how little slang the conversation needs. Léa speaks casually, but most sentences are ordinary French. The slang sits at the emotional points: a complaint, a judgement, a reaction to a spilt ice cream. That is the usual pattern. Native speakers do not switch into a separate language; they add flavour where the feeling is strongest.
