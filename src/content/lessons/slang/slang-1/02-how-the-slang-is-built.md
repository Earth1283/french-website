# How the Slang Is Built

Slang rarely breaks the rules of French. It bends them in predictable places. Once you see the grammar behind each expression, you can use it without memorising it as a single block.

## *Zut* does not change

*Zut* is an interjection, so it has no conjugation, no gender and no plural. It sits on its own, usually followed by an exclamation mark.

## *Nul* agrees with the subject

*Nul* is an adjective and must agree with what it describes: *c'est nul* for a masculine idea, *c'est nulle* for a feminine one, such as *cette idée est nulle*. When describing yourself, the feminine form appears in *Je suis nulle en maths*. Used with *en*, it means "hopeless at": *Je suis nul en maths.*

## *Laisse tomber* is an informal command

*Laisser* means "to let," and *tomber* means "to fall." Combined, *laisser tomber* means "let it fall," or "drop it." Because it is the *tu* imperative of an *-er* verb, the final *-s* disappears: *laisse*, not *laisses*. The formal version is *laissez tomber*.

## *T'as* is *tu as*

In speech, *tu as* contracts to *t'as*. The phrase *avoir l'air* takes an adjective that agrees with the subject: *elle a l'air fatiguée*, with the feminine ending. Notice that the *e* of *tu* and the *a* of *as* merge into a single syllable.

## *Sympa* never changes

*Sympa* is short for *sympathique*. It ends in *-a*, so it never changes for gender: *un mec sympa*, *une fille sympa*. In the plural it takes a silent *-s*: *des gens sympas*.

## *Galère* is a noun

In *c'est galère*, the word works as an adjective, but *la galère* is a feminine noun meaning "the nightmare." Both forms are common, and the difference is only a matter of emphasis.
